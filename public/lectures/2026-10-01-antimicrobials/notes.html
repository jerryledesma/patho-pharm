1 · will this drug work? selective toxicity & the targets

Recording note: slides 1–3 (title, outline, objectives) and the first lines of slide 4 weren't recorded. The principles section (slides 4–22) comes from the two shorter recordings, likely from the end of the previous class.
the drug must… (slide 4)
1act against the suspected or identified organism
2reach the site of infection — "what if it's within the central nervous system? We need a drug that can cross into the CNS"
3stay active despite the organism's resistance mechanisms
4be appropriate for this patient — e.g. kidney damage → avoid the nephrotoxic drugs if we can

Choosing an antimicrobial takes more than matching a drug name to an infection.

selective toxicity — the drug acts more strongly against the microorganism than against the person receiving it, by exploiting structures or processes that differ between bacterial and human cells.
✎ her contrast: cancer is harder to treat "because the cancer cells are the human body."
key concept · selective ≠ harmless
a drug can hit bacteria well and still cause an allergic reaction, damage human tissue, or disrupt the normal flora (microbiome).
her aside on microbiome research: "all those years of anxiety, was it just the bugs?"
drug target (slide 5)why it can be selective
bacterial cell wallhuman cells don't have one
bacterial ribosomesdiffer from human cytoplasmic ribosomes
metabolic pathwaysbacteria must make some substances (folic acid — they can't take it from the environment), and a drug can block the pathway
DNA-replication enzymessome bacterial enzymes differ from human ones
cell membranedisrupting it kills bacteria, but it's similar enough to ours that human toxicity limits these drugs
slide 6 picture — "just a nice picture"
a bacterium labeled with the target sites: cell wall (blocks peptidoglycan production) · plasma membrane (cell membrane, or LPS of the gram-negative outer membrane) · ribosomes (70S — blocks protein synthesis) · DNA / RNA synthesis (blocks replication, transcription) · metabolic pathways (competes with bacterial enzymes)
the four groups we study (slide 7)
  • cell wall synthesis: penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, vancomycin
  • protein synthesis (ribosomes): tetracyclines, macrolides, clindamycin, linezolid, aminoglycosides
  • folate synthesis: sulfonamides + trimethoprim
  • DNA replication / integrity: fluoroquinolones, metronidazole

A shared target ≠ identical coverage, resistance patterns or adverse effects.

2 · narrow vs broad, and how bacteria win

narrow spectrum
active against a smaller group of organisms · "the best choice for our patients if possible" — kills the bug we want and very little normal flora · excellent when the pathogen is known and susceptible
broad spectrum
active against a wider range · useful when the organism is unknown (usually where we are at first) or several must be covered — e.g. a ruptured appendix spills many gut bacteria into the peritoneal cavity · cost: more normal flora killed → diarrhea, yeast infections, C. diff, and more selection pressure
key concept (slide 8)
broad spectrum does not mean "more powerful." It covers more kinds of bacteria, and may be a poorer choice than a targeted drug for a particular infection.
FYI only — slide 9 spectrum chart: "You do not need to memorize spectrums… we're not going to ask you to figure that out." (Penicillin G is super narrow, mainly gram-positive strep; cefepime covers almost everything; "MRSA has a nice big hole.")

"Our drugs are the weapons, and now they are creating new shields."

resistance mechanism (slide 10)what happens to the drug
drug inactivationa bacterial enzyme breaks down or modifies the drug
target alterationthe drug reaches the bacterium but binds poorly — the target's shape or charge has changed
reduced entrytoo little drug gets inside
effluxthe bacterium pumps the drug back out
selection pressure — her story (slides 11–12)
bacteria mutate constantly (spontaneous mutation) → one randomly gets an efflux pump
drug given → kills everything else → the resistant one survives
no competition for food or space → "reproduce like crazy" → spreads to other people

Microbes secrete compounds toxic to each other and compete for nutrients, so an antibiotic that removes the competitors favors the resistant organism. Without the drug, the mutant is "no more likely to survive" than the rest.

student asked: (question not audible — about resistance) → "I'm going to get to that. I have a whole slide on that" → selection pressure (slides 11–12).
key concepts (slides 11–12)
the bacteria become resistant — not the patient.
Exposure doesn't "teach" a bacterium to resist; resistant bacteria may already be there, and the drug favors their survival.
  • all antimicrobials can promote resistance — broad spectrum most likely
  • the more antibiotics used, the faster resistant organisms emerge
  • stewardship: no antibiotics for viral infections; be judicious about how and when
  • bacteria share the trick: "hey buddy, look what I found… you can make this for yourself too"
FYI only — "you really don't need to know the ins and outs of transformation (naked DNA taken up), transduction (bacteriophage carries it) and conjugation ('cell sex' through a pilus)."

3 · cultures BEFORE the first dose: C&S, empiric → targeted

✎ her pearl: "I know I'll say it multiple times throughout the year… Once the first dose of antibiotics goes into the patient, then our results are not accurate." If you're hanging the first dose, make sure the ordered cultures have been drawn — as long as it doesn't delay treatment.
1collect ordered cultures — wound, blood, urine, sputum ("match the drug with the bug")
2Gram stain — gram-positive vs gram-negative, back in about 15–20 min
3start empiric (often broad) therapy — don't wait for results, especially in sepsis: "start broad, but then we tailor"
4culture grows out in 24–48 h; susceptibility shows which drugs are likely to inhibit the isolated organism
5reassess — continue, narrow, change or stop = targeted therapy
the petri dish (slide 13 picture)
disks soaked in different antibiotics on a plate of the patient's organism: the biggest clear circles = most effective. A drug with no circle is a poor choice for this strain. But if the two best are highly nephrotoxic and the patient has kidney damage, go down the list. The infectious-disease team helps — "pick their brains."
nursing (slide 14)
  • check whether ordered specimens have been obtained
  • use the correct collection technique — "the purest sample possible" (cath urine cleaning, extra skin prep for blood draws)
  • document specimen and antimicrobial administration times
  • follow up on results that come back after treatment starts — a new result may change the drug
empiric therapy (slide 15) — started before the organism is known; "the best guess," drawing on: the suspected source and likely organisms · severity and urgency · local resistance information and guidelines · the patient's allergy history, organ function and other factors. Sepsis → "multiple broad spectrum antibiotics as quick as possible."
targeted therapy — changing based on culture and sensitivity (and the patient's response).
beyond the lab (slide 16)
  • site — can enough drug get there? CNS, bone and walled-off abscesses are hard
  • patient — serious reaction history? kidney or liver dysfunction? pregnancy? another medication?
  • source — an abscess or infected device may need an intervention as well as a drug

"Susceptible" describes the organism and drug in the lab; the decision must also fit the site and the patient. The first prescription is a starting decision — new information can change it.

4 · superinfection, duration, combination, prophylaxis + nursing basics

superinfection (slide 17)
a new infection during treatment of the first: antibiotics kill the normal flora that keep other bugs in check, so a second agent flourishes — C. difficile, fungal infections. More common with broad-spectrum drugs and long courses. May need more treatment and reassessment of the original drug.
duration (slide 18)
  • take it exactly as prescribed; don't skip doses, shorten treatment or save medication
  • contact the prescriber before stopping for an adverse effect or concern
  • the team may appropriately shorten, change or stop therapy after reassessment
✎ the old "always finish all your antibiotics" is out — "really just follow the directions," because C&S results may change the plan.
combination (slide 19)details
whysevere infection (sepsis; ruptured appendix — "triple antibiotics usually") · mixed infection (more than one organism) · preventing resistance — tuberculosis ("at least two months") · enhanced antibacterial action
downsidesmore toxic and allergic reactions · possible antagonism · more superinfection · more drug-resistant bacteria · cost
prophylaxis (slide 20)
selected surgical procedures (gut or CNS surgery — infection risk outweighs the downside) · selected patients at highest risk of infective endocarditis before certain dental procedures · profound immunosuppression.
Indication, drug, timing and duration must be specific — surgical prophylaxis is a few doses or days, "not several weeks."
side effects of almost every antibiotic (slide 21)

nausea, vomiting · diarrhea and altered GI flora (even IV drugs) · yeast infections (oral, skin folds, vaginal, groin) · superinfection

BEFORE THE FIRST DOSE
confirm the drug and indication · clarify the reaction history ("Oh, I got diarrhea." — that's a side effect, not an allergy) · patient factors: kidney, liver, pregnancy, other meds, baseline symptoms · specimens collected? If urgent, communicate — don't independently delay a time-sensitive dose
DURING
give at the ordered interval and route, on time — off schedule can drop the level out of the therapeutic range · watch response and adverse effects · review new C&S with the team · recognize when the picture calls for reassessment
TEACH
"Take this medication exactly as prescribed. Contact your prescriber if you have a concerning effect or think you need to stop it. Do not save leftover antibiotics or share them."

5 · break the wall: β-lactams, PBPs, β-lactamase & the MRSA field trip

why the wall (slide 24)

Bacteria build a strong wall of peptidoglycan: it keeps their shape, resists osmotic pressure and stops them rupturing as water enters. Humans have no peptidoglycan wall → selective toxicity.

Her picture: the inside of a bacterium is very concentrated, so once the wall weakens "water is just going to flow in… we force it to blow up" = osmotic lysis.

patho → pharm (slide 25)
wall needs PBPs → β-lactam blocks PBPs → wall weakens → bacterium dies
  • β-lactams = penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems (3 of the 4 cell-wall groups)
  • bind penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which cross-link peptidoglycan
  • bactericidal · work best on actively growing bacteria
FYI only — the β-lactam ring structure: "you don't need to know what it looks like." It's the active part that binds the PBP.
how bacteria fight back (slide 26)
  • efflux pumps push penicillin out before it reaches the PBPs
  • cell wall changes (thicker) reduce access to the PBPs
  • β-lactamase (penicillinase) production
  • altered PBPs — "the key no longer fits into the lock"
β-lactamases = penicillinases (slide 27)
enzymes that cleave the β-lactam ring → the drug can't bind the PBP. Those that work on penicillin are called penicillinases. Gained by mutation or "picked up from a friend." Her line — the bacterium says "No wait, I've got a weapon."
field trip: MRSA (slides 28–29)
S. aureus: gram-positive; colonizes skin and nostrils of ~30% of people. Infections are usually skin/soft tissue — abscess, boil ("a big ol' pimple"), cellulitis (infection under the skin), impetigo (face, around the mouth); lung or blood infections are more often fatal.
MRSA = altered PBPs with low affinity for most β-lactams — "the locks have been changed at all the doors."
HCA-MRSA: elderly, recent surgery/hospitalization, dialysis, prolonged antibiotics, indwelling catheter, long-term care · CA-MRSA: contact-sport athletes, MSM, close quarters (prisons, military, college dorms) · spread person to person → contact isolation
student asked: does the β-lactam work on MRSA? → no — "the β-lactam ring cannot bind to the PBP, so it's basically ineffective."
student asked: how common is MRSA — are healthcare workers carriers? → ~30% carry S. aureus but only ~1–2% carry MRSA; "I would guess that a lot of healthcare workers are colonized." (Her story: the hospital wouldn't let her swab the shared thermometers.)
student asked: can colonizing staph turn into an infection? → yes, any colonizing staph can infect broken skin; a MRSA strain is just harder to treat.
she asked: "Why is MRSA such a big deal? Why do we have such a hard time attacking it?" → our biggest gun works on PBPs, and MRSA has totally different PBPs.

6 · penicillins, β-lactamase inhibitors & "allergic to penicillin?"

all penicillins (slide 31)
  • bind PBPs, inhibit cell wall synthesis · bactericidal against growing bacteria
  • can cause hypersensitivity (anaphylaxis risk "is well known")
  • disrupt normal flora → superinfection
  • differ mainly in spectrum and resistance to β-lactamases
  • generic names end in -cillin
the 3 you're responsible forhigh-yield connection
amoxicillincommon oral penicillin
amoxicillin/clavulanate (Augmentin)adds protection from selected β-lactamases · common oral · take with food
piperacillin/tazobactam (Zosyn)broad, IV, "one of the most common IV antibiotics" in the hospital · covers Pseudomonas
β-lactamase inhibitors (slide 32)
clavulanate and tazobactam block selected β-lactamases → the paired penicillin stays intact and reaches its PBP.
"We came out with our guns, the bacteria got their shields… inhibitors can break through the shields." Given alone they do nothing.
β-lactamase made → inhibitor blocks it → penicillin intact → reaches PBP
penicillin nursing (slide 34)
  • watch: hypersensitivity/anaphylaxis (even with no prior reaction) · nausea, diarrhea · flora disruption · C. diff · neurotoxicity/seizures with excessive concentrations
  • before: clarify allergy · review cultures and the reason · kidney function (toxicity risk with renal impairment) · methotrexate — some penicillins reduce its elimination → toxicity
  • during: response, hypersensitivity, significant diarrhea, reassess broad therapy when results return
  • teach: exactly as prescribed · don't share or save · report breathing trouble, swelling, severe rash, significant diarrhea · Augmentin with food — "their bellies will feel less sad"

Methotrexate (spring): "basically poison… small changes can make a big difference."

penicillin allergy (slide 33)
10% report it · only 1% truly allergic.
"An itchy rash all over… is very different from my throat closed and I couldn't breathe."
ask
  • which medication caused it?
  • what exactly happened?
  • how soon after the dose?
  • how long ago?
  • tolerated a penicillin or cephalosporin since?

True allergy to one penicillin = all penicillins · some cross-sensitivity with cephalosporins · anaphylaxis risk very low but life-threatening · desensitization if there's no other option.

✎ breathing difficulty, mouth/airway swelling, hypotension, hives → STOP THE INFUSION and notify the provider.
student asked: the allergy is from childhood — "my mom told me I was allergic"? → clarify it; most of the time we just pick something other than a penicillin.
student asked: what if penicillin is the only option (e.g. syphilis)? → ICU admission and desensitization, ready for anaphylaxis.
student asked: is C. diff contagious to healthy people? → it's "really hard to get C. diff if you have a normal flora"; it takes hold when the flora is disrupted (a student got it after heavy sinus-infection antibiotics).
student asked: how do you treat C. diff? → more antibiotics — which disrupt the flora more; fecal transplant is being researched.
student asked: penicillin neurotoxicity? → very high concentrations can cause neurotoxicity or seizures — "pretty uncommon" at the doses we give.
FYI only — C. diff precautions: contact plus; wash hands with soap and water (foam doesn't kill it); bleach wipes for equipment. "This is really more like lab-type stuff, so I'm not going to ask you."

7 · cephalosporins (ceftriaxone + calcium) & carbapenems (valproic acid)

cephalosporins (slides 36–37)
  • like penicillins: β-lactam ring, bind PBPs, bactericidal, broad, most active on growing cells
  • inactivated by β-lactamases (cephalosporinases)
  • names start with cef- or ceph- — "that is your clue"
  • prototype: ceftriaxone (Rocephin), a third-generation cephalosporin
recognizewhy
cefazolinsurgical prophylaxis
cefepimecovers Pseudomonas
ceftarolinebinds MRSA's altered PBP → MRSA — "ceftaroline got weird with it"
FYI only — names and generations: "you do not need to memorize all these names… we're not even going to worry about [generations]." There are over 20, in five generations.
student asked: do cephalosporins bind the same PBPs? → yes, same target — the rest of the molecule is different enough to be a separate class.
student asked: generic or trade names on the exam? → always the generic; the trade name too if she taught it.
student asked: severe penicillin allergy — give a cephalosporin instead? → there's crossover risk; first look for something with no risk, but it may be considered if the infection is severe.
cephalosporin nursing (slide 38)
  • allergic reaction — rash to anaphylaxis (PCN cross-allergy!)
  • thrombophlebitis — irritating to veins: dilute, give slowly IV
  • hemolytic anemia — rare antibody destruction of RBCs
  • diarrhea — could be C. diff ("that's a quite recognizable odor")
  • before: clarify any previous penicillin or cephalosporin reaction (risk depends on the drug and the reaction) · review the drug, site, cultures, kidney function
  • monitor: hypersensitivity, GI effects, C. diff, renal/neuro toxicity when relevant, response
ceftriaxone + calcium-containing IV solutions
forms a precipitate — crystal deposits in the lungs and kidneys; "has killed some babies" (neonatal restrictions are especially strict).
Never run two things in one line without checking IV compatibility: use separate lumens, or give one, stop, flush, then give the other.
student asked: only one IV line? → separate lumens, or give, stop, flush, then give sequentially.
student asked: shouldn't this be caught earlier? → EMR alerts and pharmacy checks should — but think Swiss cheese: you're the last layer.
student asked: why is it dangerous in the line but not the body? → concentration — it's diluted once it's in the bloodstream; infants have "a lot less body to distribute through."
carbapenems (slide 39) — prototype imipenem/cilastatin (Primaxin)
  • broadest-spectrum β-lactams · good for mixed infections · particularly effective against anaerobes · can get through the gram-negative cell envelope
  • NOT effective against MRSA
  • cilastatin blocks the kidney's enzyme that inactivates imipenem — "fighting against the body's metabolism," not the bacteria
  • adverse: superinfection · headache, confusion, seizures
  • kidney impairment ↑ imipenem exposure → ↑ seizure risk
  • carbapenems can markedly lower valproic acid (a seizure medication) → breakthrough seizures — "one of the biggest risks for having a huge, horrible seizure"
  • nursing: review kidney function and dose · assess neuro status · identify valproic acid use · narrow when microbiology returns

8 · vancomycin: route matters, "put together vanco and kidneys"

vancomycin (Vancocin) — a glycopeptide, not a β-lactam. Binds peptidoglycan building blocks and blocks wall construction · bactericidal · "the most commonly given antibiotic in the hospital."
  • works on gram-positives including MRSA — MRSA's altered PBPs don't matter, because vanco "works elsewhere" in wall construction
IV vanco
systemic therapy for serious gram-positive infections — "our big MRSA infections"
oral vanco
not absorbed — stays in the intestine → intestinal C. diff only; not reliable for systemic infection
✎ "Route is really important." Verify the route matches the infection — oral vanco for a skin infection or MRSA bacteremia won't reach it.
other adverse effects (slide 41)
  • ototoxicity — kidneys and ears share embryologic origin, so nephrotoxic drugs are often ototoxic
  • infusion reaction (histamine release): flushing, pruritus, hypotension — "can look like anaphylaxis, but it really isn't" → stop, notify; future doses may be infused more slowly
  • IV-site irritation
"really put together vanco and kidneys"
vanco is nephrotoxic → watch serum creatinine (and BUN), urine output, other nephrotoxic drugs, ordered vanco levels.
"A trend of anything is better than a one-time reading."
student asked: why do creatinine and BUN show kidney damage? → the kidneys normally clear them, so they build up when the kidneys aren't clearing.
levelswhen drawn
trough (most used)lowest point — right before the next dose. High troughs predict kidney damage better than high peaks.
peakusually ~30 min after an IV infusion, ~1 h after an oral dose
  • give IV doses at the prescribed rate — over about 1–1.5 h, never fast
  • draw levels at the specified time
  • monitor IV site, infusion tolerance, kidney function, response
  • report hearing changes, severe diarrhea, ↓ urine output, treatment failure

9 · protein synthesis I: static vs cidal, doxycycline, azithromycin

targeting the ribosome (slide 44)

Bacteria need proteins to build structures, run metabolism, grow and reproduce. Bacterial ribosomes differ from human ones, so these drugs can be selective — but they still cause significant adverse effects and interactions.

FYI-level — "You don't need to know… 70S… 80S. No, I don't care about the numbers… Just know that it's the ribosomes." The slides and charts list them: bacterial 70S vs human 80S · 30S: tetracyclines (doxycycline), aminoglycosides (gentamicin) · 50S: macrolides (azithromycin), clindamycin, linezolid.
bacteriostatic
suppresses growth and reproduction; the immune system finishes the job — "functioning immune system required!" · doxycycline, azithromycin, clindamycin, linezolid (against many organisms)
bactericidal
kills · aminoglycosides (gentamicin) — the bacterium makes "weird, poor proteins" and dies
✎ "You really don't want to use bacteriostatic drugs with patients who are immunocompromised," because you're banking on the immune system to do the last bit. (Static vs cidal isn't a ranking of strength.)
doxycycline (Vibramycin) · tetracycline (slide 46)
binds the ribosome, stops amino acids being added · usually bacteriostatic
adverse: nausea, diarrhea · esophageal irritation/ulceration · photosensitivity · permanent tooth discoloration and effects on developing bone · superinfection / C. diff
interaction: aluminum, calcium, iron, magnesium (antacids, iron, calcium, magnesium products) bind it → ↓ absorption
before: pregnancy status and age · antacids and supplements · swallowing ability, esophageal risk
teach: full glass of water · upright ≥ 30 min · not right before bed · separate from antacids/minerals · sun protection, report severe skin reactions
her rule (not on the slide)
doxycycline: never in pregnancy and never under age 7 — "their baby teeth are still developing underneath."
student asked: do antacids or minerals have to be stopped? → no — just separate them, "usually they say 2–3 hours."
azithromycin (Zithromax) · macrolide (slide 47)
ribosome · usually bacteriostatic
adverse: nausea, abdominal discomfort, diarrhea · QT prolongation → fatal dysrhythmia · hepatotoxicity · superinfection / C. diff
dysrhythmia risk ↑ with: other QT-prolonging drugs · hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia · existing rhythm disorders
before: cardiac history, medications, QT risk factors, liver · report: palpitations, syncope (fainting), rhythm changes · jaundice, dark urine
✎ her pearl: "Women are automatically at increased risk, because at baseline we have a longer QT interval."
FYI only — common uses (tick-borne infections, chlamydia, acne, atypical respiratory organisms…): "you don't need to memorize all these common uses… you guys aren't responsible for prescribing."

Her antibiotic fatigue: "another antibiotic, and another antibiotic… I promise autonomic nervous system is way cooler."

10 · protein synthesis II: clinda → C. diff, linezolid (MRSA/VRE), gentamicin

CLINDAMYCIN (Cleocin)
lincosamide · usually bacteriostatic
"has really fallen out of favor" — super high correlation with C. diff-associated diarrhea and colitis
also: nausea, rash, hepatotoxicity · may enhance neuromuscular blockers
LINEZOLID (Zyvox)
oxazolidinone · blocks the protein-synthesis initiation complex
kept for MRSA and VRE (vancomycin-resistant enterococci) — "let's protect this antibiotic… if your patient is on this, they probably have one of these, or maybe both"
GENTAMICIN (Garamycin)
aminoglycoside · bactericidal — misreads mRNA → abnormal proteins
serious aerobic gram-negative infections; sometimes combined for enhanced action
nephrotoxic + ototoxic
✎ "If nothing else, just be like, clinda → C. diff."
clindamycin nursing (slide 48)
  • before: indication and cultures · previous antibiotic-associated diarrhea or colitis · bowel pattern ("what is normal for you") · other meds
  • during and after: new or worse diarrhea → report watery or bloody stools, cramping, fever
  • don't independently give an antidiarrheal for significant diarrhea
linezolid (slide 49)
  • myelosuppression, especially thrombocytopenia → CBC as ordered (commonly weekly); assess bruising, bleeding (gums, urine, oozing IV site), pallor, infection
  • MAO-inhibitor activity → serotonin syndrome with SSRIs/SNRIs and other psych/serotonergic meds (also some TCAs, buspirone, some opioids, lots of tyramine-rich food) → careful medication reconciliation
  • signs to watch: agitation, confusion, diaphoresis, tremor, fever, muscle rigidity
  • also nausea, diarrhea, headache
FYI — "Don't worry so much about the neuropathy. Serotonin syndrome, you can put that in your back pocket" (spring). Know only that linezolid + psych meds interact, and the signs. Peripheral/optic neuropathy comes with prolonged therapy.
gentamicin safety (slides 50–51)
nephrotoxicity ↑ with kidney impairment, higher exposure, longer therapy, other nephrotoxic drugs
ototoxicity — may be irreversible: tinnitus, hearing loss, dizziness, vertigo, balance problems
neuromuscular blockade — may worsen weakness or enhance neuromuscular blockers
  • review kidney function, dose, other nephrotoxic/ototoxic drugs
  • assess baseline hearing, balance, neuromuscular concerns
  • monitor creatinine, urine output
  • draw gentamicin levels at the specified times; coordinate dose and lab timing; document times
  • report new tinnitus, hearing change, dizziness, vertigo, balance trouble
Not tested — weight-based dosing ("I'm not going to ask a question about this") and the loop diuretics ("don't worry about the diuretics so much right now").

11 · DNA & folate: cipro, metronidazole, TMP-SMX

Bacteria must copy and maintain DNA, and must make their own folate (for DNA, RNA, some proteins). Humans get folate from the diet → selective toxicity (slides 54, 58).

ciprofloxacin (Cipro) · fluoroquinolone (slides 55–56)
blocks bacterial DNA enzymes (DNA gyrase, topoisomerase IV) → DNA replication fails · bactericidal · covers susceptible Pseudomonas, complicated UTIs and more
serious: tendinitis / tendon rupture (usually the Achilles — "significant surgery and recovery") · peripheral neuropathy · CNS effects · dysglycemia · QT prolongation
other: N/D · photosensitivity · C. diff
tendon risk ↑: older age · corticosteroids · kidney, heart or lung transplant · kidney impairment
interactions: Al, Ca, Fe, Mg, zinc (antacids, supplements, multivitamins) ↓ absorption — separate by several hours · tizanidine contraindicated ("should never be given together") · other QT drugs
teach: separate from minerals · hydrate · sun protection · report tendon pain or swelling promptly · report numbness, tingling, burning pain, weakness, confusion, severe dizziness
her rule (not on the slide)
"We don't like to give it to people under the age of 18 because of this risk of tendon rupture."
metronidazole (Flagyl) · nitroimidazole (slide 57)
enters anaerobes, becomes reactive compounds that damage DNA · bactericidal · "a super common drug" in the hospital
adverse: nausea · metallic taste · headache · harmless darkened urine (warn them!) · peripheral neuropathy with prolonged use · rare seizures or encephalopathy
interactions: alcohol and propylene glycol · warfarin (↑ anticoagulant effect) · disulfiram
nursing: neuro status · bleeding and INR with warfarin · diarrhea · report numbness, tingling, trouble walking, confusion, seizures
no alcohol: during + ≥ 3 days after
it mimics Antabuse (disulfiram) — "if you drink it, you get sick." Enough drug stays in the system for 3 days after the last dose.

Warfarin: "We keep hearing the same warfarin. We finally get to it, I promise."

TMP-SMX (Bactrim, Septra) · folate antagonist (slides 58–60)
sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim block two sequential steps of bacterial folate synthesis → stronger together; generally bactericidal
common: N/V, rash, photosensitivity
serious: Stevens-Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis (can be fatal) · blood dyscrasias, marrow suppression · hyperkalemia · kidney injury, crystalluria · severe hepatic reactions
higher risk: kidney impairment, folate deficiency, older age, long or high-dose treatment, other K⁺-raising meds
interactions: warfarin (↑ bleeding → watch INR) · other drugs that raise K⁺ (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, K⁺-sparing diuretics, K⁺ supplements)
before: ask about sulfa (sulfonamide) reactions · kidney function and K⁺ · CBC · warfarin or K⁺-raising drugs?
during: rash or mucosal lesions promptly · K⁺, kidney function, CBC, INR
teach: drink plenty of fluid (crystals) unless contraindicated (heart or liver failure) · sun protection · report rash, bleeding, weakness, palpitations, ↓ urine

12 · antifungals (fluconazole, ampho B, nystatin) + oseltamivir

why fungi are hard (slide 63)
  • fungi are eukaryotic like us → fewer unique targets, more toxicity, long treatment
  • targets: fungal membranes use ergosterol (not cholesterol) · fungal wall has β-glucan
  • azoles (fluconazole) inhibit ergosterol synthesis · polyenes (amphotericin B, nystatin) bind ergosterol and disrupt the membrane
fluconazole (Diflucan) · azole (slide 64)
blocks a fungal enzyme that makes ergosterol → leaky membrane · fungistatic (the immune system finishes) · Candida: oral, esophageal, vaginal; selected systemic · other azoles are OTC for athlete's foot and vaginal yeast
adverse: GI, headache · hepatotoxicity · QT prolongation
interactions: inhibits liver drug enzymes → ↑ effects of other drugs — warfarin (bleeding) · other QT drugs
nursing: med list · baseline LFTs (serial if long) · INR with warfarin · QT risks · teach: report jaundice, dark urine, bleeding, rash, palpitations, fainting
amphotericin B · polyene (slides 65–66)
binds ergosterol → pores → leakage → fungicidal · severe systemic/invasive infections, often in severely immunosuppressed cancer patients · ~6–8 weeks IV · "a nasty, nasty, nasty drug"
infusion reactions (esp. early doses): fever, shaking chills, N/V, headache, hypotension, tachypnea — "like the flu times 10. It is miserable. It is not deadly."
adverse: nephrotoxicity · hypokalemia · hypomagnesemia · anemia · ↑ liver enzymes · phlebitis
nursing: baseline vitals, watch closely during infusion · prescribed rate · premedications only when ordered · IV site · verify the exact formulation · follow protocol, notify for significant reactions
monitor: creatinine & BUN · K⁺ & Mg²⁺ · I&O · CBC · LFTs · other nephrotoxic drugs · report ↓ urine output or ↑ creatinine · muscle weakness, cramping, dysrhythmias
the meme (slide 65)
Nephron: "What happened?" — "Amphotericin B." "You can remember it, right?"
nystatin (Mycostatin) · polyene (slide 67)
binds ergosterol · acts locally — inactivated once it hits stomach acid, so it's "really a topical medication" · oral candidiasis (thrush) and skin infections (her son's diaper rash) · not for systemic infection · few interactions (minimal absorption)
teach: shake and measure the suspension · swish it around the mouth and hold before swallowing · oral hygiene, denture care, finish the course
She crossed out slide 66's digoxin bullet: "Don't even worry about hypokalemia with digoxin toxicity… just cross that out." Also FYI: the azole enzyme details and the antiviral target list.
oseltamivir (Tamiflu) (slides 69–73)
viruses reproduce inside our cells → few safe targets · antivirals suppress replication, they don't kill · -vir = antiviral
inhibits neuraminidase → new influenza viruses can't be released to spread · treats influenza A and B (and prevention after selected exposures) — not the common cold, bacterial infections, or every flu-like illness
timing: start ASAP, within 48 h of symptoms — "within 48 hours is really the cutoff"; severe, hospitalized or high-risk patients may still benefit later · it shortens and lessens symptoms, doesn't cure
adverse: nausea, vomiting, headache (take with food) · rare skin, hypersensitivity, neuropsychiatric reactions (esp. teens)
may interfere with the live attenuated intranasal flu vaccine · not a vaccine — you can get both
nursing: when did symptoms start? · severity and risk factors · renal function (dose) · hydration · mental status
