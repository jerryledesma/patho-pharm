1 · how clotting normally happens

Hemostasis stops bleeding after vessel injury while keeping blood flowing elsewhere (slide 4). Platelets form the first plug; fibrin stabilizes it. You need both.

stepwhat happenskey components
1 · vascular responsevessel constricts to reduce blood lossvascular smooth muscle
2 · primary hemostasisplatelets adhere, activate and aggregate → initial plugplatelets, von Willebrand factor, ADP, thromboxane A₂
3 · secondary hemostasiscoagulation makes thrombin, which turns fibrinogen into fibrinfactor Xa, thrombin, fibrin
4 · fibrinolysisthe fibrin clot breaks down as healing progressesplasminogen, plasmin
primary: platelets (slide 5)

Injury exposes subendothelial collagen and von Willebrand factor.

1adhesion: platelets attach to the injured surface
2activation: they change shape and release ADP and TXA₂
3aggregation: activated GP IIb/IIIa receptors bind fibrinogen, bridging platelets

Activation is "a song of all of the information coming in" before a platelet decides. Two pathways we drug: COX-1 → TXA₂, and ADP → P2Y12.

secondary: the cascade (slide 6)
factor Xa → prothrombin → thrombin → fibrinogen → fibrin mesh
  • intrinsic and extrinsic pathways meet at the common pathway
  • factor Xa is the central enzyme in thrombin generation
  • thrombin also amplifies coagulation
  • some clotting factors need vitamin K to form
FYI: she said not to memorize this: "You do not need to memorize the entire cascade," or "which clotting factors they are" (slides 6 and 17 list II, VII, IX, X; 00:04:32). Study guide ¶8 and ¶24 still ask about them.
antithrombin · limits clotting
Natural anticoagulant protein. Inactivates thrombin (IIa) and factor Xa, keeping the clot localized.
drug link: heparin and enoxaparin enhance it
plasmin · breaks down fibrin
Circulates as inactive plasminogen. Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) converts it to plasmin.
drug link: alteplase makes more plasmin

3 · heparin vs Lovenox

Anticoagulants prevent clot formation or extension. They don't directly dissolve a clot. They keep it from growing, so "the body's own slow mechanism" can break it down, sometimes over several months.

student asked: "Would heparin and Lovenox be given at the same time?" → no. They're similar in structure; "we wouldn't give them at the same time."
when each one fits (slide 15)
  • UFH: rapid anticoagulation, or rapid discontinuation may be needed, like a possible big surgery. It's also cheap and suits the hospital, where nurses are there 24 hours.
  • Lovenox: predictable; DVT prophylaxis and treatment. Preferred outpatient: no lab trips, one less injection a day.
kidneys ✎
Anticoagulant levels climb in renal disease, and "it's particularly severe and dangerous." Enoxaparin is substantially cleared by the kidneys.

4 · HIT: low platelets, more clots

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is an immune reaction to heparin: thrombocytopenia plus increased thrombosis risk. "The primary danger is thrombosis, not hemorrhage" (slide 16).

heparin binds platelet factor 4 (PF4) → antibodies form against the complex → platelets activate inappropriately → hypercoagulable + platelets used up
recognize it
  • platelet count falls, often by ≥ 50% from baseline
  • usually 5–10 days after starting heparin; timing varies with prior exposure
  • ↑ DVT, PE, stroke, MI
  • falling platelets are the earliest sign, so monitor them on heparin
respond
  • stop all heparin products
  • non-heparin anticoagulant, since thrombosis risk stays high
  • can't just switch to Lovenox: "there's still an overlap"
  • treated as a heparin allergy, forever
✎ her pearl: platelets "activate at the drop of a hat" and go "haywire." Bleeding can happen once you've used them up, "but in general, what we're really worried about is the excess of clotting."
the paradox
Low platelets usually make you think bleeding. In HIT, think clots.

5 · warfarin: slow start · INR 2–3

Warfarin (Coumadin, Jantoven), an oral anticoagulant, inhibits vitamin K recycling (VKORC1), so the liver makes fewer functional vitamin K-dependent clotting factors. Cut a few and the whole cascade suffers. It also lowers proteins C and S.

why it's slow

Clotting factors live for several days, so old ones keep working while new ones stop forming. Therapeutic effect takes several days.

Heparin bridging: start heparin and warfarin together. Heparin anticoagulates right away and continues until warfarin reaches range, so a stroke doesn't happen in the gap.

main uses (slide 17)
  • mechanical heart valves: "they have to be on warfarin"
  • AF stroke prevention: the most common place you'll see it; about 19% of anticoagulated patients still use it
  • occasionally DVT/PE, "if it's super big"

Doses vary hugely between people, so expect frequent checks and constant adjustment until stable.

student asked: "Can Lovenox be used for heparin bridging?" → "No, because we need the drip." Clot risk is high, so they're often hospitalized for this part.
student asked: "Why warfarin rather than heparin for AFib?" → route (injection vs oral), and it's much harder to reach that degree of anticoagulation with heparin injections.

6 · warfarin: consistency, interactions, reversal

vitamin K: consistent, not avoided
↑ vitamin K (leafy greens) → ↓ warfarin effect → ↑ clot risk
↓ vitamin K → ↑ warfarin effect → ↑ bleeding

"The consistency is the key." Don't suddenly "eat all spinach salads in one week."

drug interactions
  • aspirin, NSAIDs: add bleeding risk
  • warfarin is about 99% protein-bound. A new protein-bound drug (many antibiotics, antifungals) knocks it free → more anticoagulation
  • medication changes may need extra INR checks and dose changes
  • some supplements and OTC products too
teaching
  • take it consistently, as prescribed
  • go to INR appointments
  • report unusual bleeding, bruising, melena, hematuria
  • tell every healthcare professional before procedures or new medications
  • contraindicated in pregnancy: fetal harm

Her heartbreaking case: uninsured, no follow-up tests, INR "like 11 point something," hemorrhagic stroke.

Reversal depends on the INR, whether there's bleeding, and how severe. A high INR without active bleeding may just mean holding warfarin for a while.

optionhow it worksspeed
vitamin K (phytonadione)restores the liver's ability to make functional factorsnot immediate; new factors must be made, sustained
4F-PCC ("way easier to say")IV concentrate of the four deficient factorsrapid, for serious or life-threatening bleeding
fresh frozen plasmaall clotting factors; "kind of a waste of plasma" when only four are neededlarger volumes, slower; if PCC is unavailable
student asked: "Why give vitamin K along with 4F-PCC?" → PCC replaces the factors for now, but they only last a little while. Vitamin K lets the liver build its own over the next several days.

7 · DOACs: easier, but timing is everything

better than warfarin
  • rapid onset, predictable effect
  • no routine INR monitoring
  • fewer dietary interactions
  • often preferred for eligible AF and many DVT/PE patients
the catch
  • bleeding is still the main adverse effect
  • short half-lives: a missed or even late dose reduces protection
  • kidney function and interactions affect dosing
  • not for mechanical valves
  • warfarin stays preferred for moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis with AF
✎ her pearl: explain why. "If you don't take it exactly on time, then you are going to be at increased risk of clots and having a stroke."
dabigatran (Pradaxa): direct thrombin inhibitor
  • binds thrombin (IIa), so fibrinogen can't become fibrin
  • mainly nonvalvular AF stroke prevention; selected VTE
  • bleeding, including GI; dyspepsia
  • significant renal elimination
  • reversal: idarucizumab (Praxbind)

Pradaxa and Praxbind sound alike. Memory hook: Prax-bind binds it.

apixaban (Eliquis), rivaroxaban (Xarelto): direct Xa inhibitors
block Xa → ↓ thrombin → ↓ fibrin
  • AF stroke prevention, DVT/PE treatment and prevention
  • renal function and interacting drugs affect dosing
  • no direct reversal agent in this lecture: stop the drug, supportive care, consider 4F-PCC or plasma
Xa + ban "Pretty cool, huh?"
  • Xablocks factor Xa; Xarelto even has it in the name
  • banapixaban, rivaroxaban
student asked: "How do you handle timing when a patient travels across time zones?" → talk through how it affects their life and set a smartphone alarm. If they can't keep the schedule (shift work, heavy travel), warfarin may fit better.
student asked: "Same time each dose?" → as close as possible, within about a half hour. After a missed dose, get the next one on board as soon as possible; follow that drug's instructions.
student asked: "Can patients check INR at home?" → setups exist but are expensive and harder than a glucose stick. "The home ones have not gotten to the point yet where we're feeling consistently good about them."
student asked: "What about DIC?" → not today. "We will get to DIC" with shock.

9 · antiplatelets: aspirin

plaque rupture or erosion → platelet adhesion, activation → aggregation → coronary thrombus
classdrugswhat it blocks
COX-1 inhibitoraspirin↓ thromboxane A₂ → ↓ activation
P2Y12 inhibitorsclopidogrel (Plavix), ticagrelor (Brilinta)ADP-mediated activation
GP IIb/IIIa inhibitorseptifibatide (Integrilin), tirofiban (Aggrastat)fibrinogen bridges between platelets, the last step

Aspirin or a P2Y12 inhibitor alone makes platelets "a little bit less likely" to activate. "We don't want to completely block platelets," because we still need them. After an MI, this slows a new plaque event and buys time to get help.

aspirin (ASA)
irreversibly inhibits COX-1 → ↓ TXA₂ → ↓ activation
  • platelets can't make new COX-1, so the effect lasts their lifespan: 7–10 days
  • stop 7–10 days before a planned procedure when you can; not if it's protecting a new stent
  • uses: ACS including MI, secondary prevention after MI or certain strokes, stents (with a P2Y12 inhibitor)
  • low dose, commonly 81 mg daily, when indicated
aspirin risks
  • bleeding plus gastric irritation → GI bleeding and ulcers
  • hypersensitivity in some patients
  • avoid in children and adolescents (Reye syndrome)
  • not for everyone over 50: without known risk factors, "you're getting risks without really getting a benefit"

"I hate that they call it a baby aspirin… you never give babies aspirin."

she asked: "If I call EMS and say I'm having chest pain, what do they tell people first?" → a student said nitroglycerin ("if they have nitroglycerin, absolutely"), but: take an aspirin and chew it, "not just swallow it," so it's absorbed as quickly as possible.

10 · P2Y12 · DAPT · GP IIb/IIIa

P2Y12 inhibitors: clopidogrel (Plavix), ticagrelor (Brilinta)
ADP binds P2Y12 (blocked here) → GP IIb/IIIa activates → aggregation
  • prevent coronary stent clotting; given during ACS
  • can be taken without aspirin
  • "really the only big thing": Plavix has more drug interactions (CYP450)
  • adverse effects: bleeding; ticagrelor can also cause dyspnea and bradyarrhythmias (slide 32)
FYI: she played these down: reversible vs irreversible and prodrug differences ("don't worry about, like, differences between those… It doesn't matter that much"). Study guide ¶47 still lists them. Prasugrel appears only on slide 35.
DAPT = aspirin + a P2Y12 inhibitor
  • when: after ACS (NSTEMI, STEMI) or stent placement
  • why: two separate pathways (COX-1 → TXA₂, ADP → P2Y12) suppress aggregation more
  • stent logic: a new stent is a foreign surface where platelets activate, especially before the vessel heals
  • cost: more bleeding
  • teach: never stop without the prescriber. Stopping early can cause stent thrombosis
student asked: "How long does a patient stay on DAPT?" → varies by stent type, with specific time frames; more in adult health. If that stent clogs off, "it's a giant MI."
GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors: eptifibatide (Integrilin), tirofiban (Aggrastat)
  • stop fibrinogen bridging, the final step of aggregation
  • IV, in selected ACS or PCI situations; not routine for every stent
  • given only during the high-risk procedure
  • significant bleeding, thrombocytopenia; worse with other antithrombotics
  • doesn't break down clots already there
three targets ✎
aspirin → COX-1
Plavix, Brilinta → P2Y12
Integrilin, Aggrastat → the bridge

11 · thrombolytics: alteplase

plasminogen in the clot → tPA → plasmin → fibrin degradation products → clot dissolves

Alteplase (Activase) is recombinant tPA: "a whole bunch more than the body would ever produce at once." It doesn't just prevent more fibrin. It breaks down fibrin that's already there, so "it will break down any clot in the body, anywhere."

only for tiny, specific situations
  • acute ischemic stroke: selected patients in the treatment window. Stroke symptoms → head CT first, as chest pain → EKG first
  • high-risk PE: especially with instability
  • STEMI, when timely PCI isn't available: a rural hospital without a cath lab. Never for unstable angina or NSTEMI
contraindications (slide 39)
  • active significant bleeding
  • known or suspected intracranial hemorrhage
  • certain recent intracranial injuries or procedures
  • severe uncontrolled hypertension
  • other high major-bleeding risk; timing matters too
she asked: a patient with a STEMI crashes the car because of it and has horrible injuries. "Can't give the thrombolytic, right?" → right: "If they're already bleeding, we're not going to give it."
nursing
  • confirm the indication; hemorrhagic stroke ruled out
  • assess bleeding risk and contraindications before giving it
  • monitor neuro status, blood pressure, signs of bleeding (GI, IV and procedure sites, internal)
  • sudden severe headache, neuro decline or altered mental status → think intracranial hemorrhage, the most serious complication
student asked: "What is reperfusion injury?" → restoring blood to oxygen-deprived tissue brings free radicals and inflammatory mediators. Still "way better than not reperfusing at all."
FYI only: reperfusion injury comes back with stroke.
