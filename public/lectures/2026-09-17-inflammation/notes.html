1 · stress → adaptation → injury → death

he asked: "Name the biggest stressor you can put on yourself that actually improves your life?" → exercise — a small stressor the cell adapts to. "What doesn't kill them makes them stronger."

stress the cell can handle → adaptation
too big, or too long → cell injury

  • Adaptive changes let stressed tissue survive or keep functioning (slide 4).
  • Mild, transient injury is reversible — the cell fixes itself.
  • Severe, progressive injury is irreversible — the cell dies so healthy cells can take its place and it doesn't "explode" on its neighbors.
  • Two ways to die: necrosis or apoptosis (section 4).
he asked: "When you think about adaptation, do you think of it as good or bad?" → generally good — but cells "aren't smart or dumb, they're just reacting," so some adaptations make things worse.
physiologic — normal, helpful response (weight lifting → bigger muscle; estrogen → breast/uterine growth)
pathologic / maladaptive — the cell "thinks it's doing a good thing" but harms the organ (HTN → thick ventricle)
compensatory — a pathologic event, then a physiologic response (one kidney removed → the other enlarges)
5 adaptations (slide 4)
atrophy · hypertrophy · hyperplasia · metaplasia · dysplasia
size ↓ · size ↑ · number ↑ · type changes · disordered growth

2 · the five adaptations

adaptationwhat changesphysiologicpathologic
atrophycell size ↓ (not number); whole organ can shrink; less ER, fewer mitochondria—disuse · denervation · loss of endocrine stimulation · inadequate nutrition · ischemia · aging
hypertrophycell size ↑ (not number); more ER, mitochondria, membrane; heart & kidney proneskeletal muscle with exercisecardiac hypertrophy from HTN
hyperplasiacell number ↑ (proliferation); can occur with hypertrophyhormonal: estrogen-dependent breast, uterusexcess hormones/growth factors (endometrium) · viral — warts
metaplasiaone mature cell type replaced by another, often less differentiated; reversible—chronic irritation: ciliated columnar → squamous in the trachea; can lead to cancer
dysplasiaderanged growth — cells vary in size, shape, organization; "atypical hyperplasia"—persistent severe irritation (cervix, respiratory tract); not truly adaptive; can lead to cancer
he asked: "Why is this [cardiac] hypertrophy a bad thing?" → the wall gets so thick the ventricle can't fill. Caused by HTN — the heart beats against high pressure, adapts, and makes things worse = maladaptive.
compensatory (slides 7 & 9)
  • Kidney removed → remaining kidney enlarges (compensatory hypertrophy).
  • Liver — remove 70% and it regenerates in ~2 weeks (compensatory hyperplasia).
  • Cells that don't divide (cardiac muscle, neurons) can't do hyperplasia.
he asked: "Metaplasia — in what situation, and why are they getting rid of their cilia?" → smoking. Cilia catch everything inhaled; the irritants injure the cells, so the cells drop their cilia to stop "grabbing the stuff."
he asked: "Adaptation or maladaptation?" → maladaptation — nothing is caught anymore.
he asked: "What has to happen inside the cell to switch type?" → genes up- and down-regulated — overdo it and you get cancer (same idea with HPV/warts).
✎ dysplasia can sometimes go back to metaplasia, and metaplasia back to normal — quitting smoking lets airway cells recover.
Kahoot — "name that atrophy": hand that lost its nerve supply → denervation · leg just out of a cast → disuse · menopause → loss of endocrine stimulation · shrunken brain / kidney → ischemia (both heavily vascularized, prone to vascular events).

3 · causes & mechanisms of cell injury

Recording gap: slides 17–25 weren't recorded. These notes are from the slides; he called the first few causes "kind of basic" and went through them quickly.
causes (slides 15–20)
  • Physical — mechanical/blunt force (tearing, shearing, crushing: MVAs, falls, contusions, lacerations, fractures, gunshot); extremes of temperature (burns, frostbite); electrical (burns, arrhythmias).
  • Radiation — ionizing (radiation therapy) & UV (sunburn) damage DNA/RNA and make free radicals; nonionizing (microwave) = thermal.
  • Chemical — xenobiotics (carbon tetrachloride, lead, CO, ethanol, mercury, street drugs); direct damage (chemo, drugs of abuse); hypersensitivity reactions (rash → immune organ failure).
  • Biologic (e.g., anthrax) — invasion, toxins, hypersensitivity.
  • Nutritional imbalances.
free radicals (oxidative stress)
uncharged atoms with an unpaired electron — e.g. reactive oxygen species (ROS). ~10% of O₂ metabolism is incompletely reduced; normally kept in check by antioxidants.
causes: chemicals/radiation, inflammation, O₂, aging, infection, ischemia-reperfusion (stroke, ALS, CV disease)
damage: lipid peroxidation · proteins · DNA · mitochondria
hypoxia — most common cause of cell injury
from low O₂ in air, loss/less effective hemoglobin, fewer RBCs, respiratory or CV disease, poisoned cytochromes; asphyxia (suffocation, strangulation, drowning).
ischemia = most common cause of hypoxia.
in the cell: ATP ↓ → Na⁺/K⁺ pump & Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange fail → swelling, vacuolation
ischemia-reperfusion injury (slide 26)
more injury when blood flow and O₂ come back: oxidative stress, ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺, inflammation, complement activation.
his point: about half the cells die from the ischemia itself — and another large share (the slide says 36–50%) die after flow is restored. "A lot of research is going into this."
impaired calcium homeostasis (slide 27)
cells keep cytosolic Ca²⁺ low; Ca²⁺ is a "second messenger" that turns on enzymes — too much damages the cell.

5 · lines of defense & why inflammation is nonspecific

1st · innate immunity
physical, mechanical, biochemical barriers — there before injury
2nd · inflammation
happens when there's injury
3rd · adaptive immunity
specific cells — "next week"
Innate barriers — "I'm not going to ask details… this is kind of obvious": skin; GI, GU & respiratory linings; mucus & cilia; coughing, sneezing, urination, vomiting, defecation · saliva, tears, earwax, sweat · lysozymes, antimicrobial peptides (defensins, collectins) · normal microbiome takes up the "real estate" bad bugs want.
inflammation (slides 45–46)
  • programmed response to cell injury — redness (erythema), heat, swelling (edema), pain, loss of function
  • neutralizes the threat, removes dead tissue, limits infection, prepares for healing, helps start adaptive immunity
  • first response · any vascularized tissue · rapid · cells + chemicals
  • nonspecific — "no matter the injury, it's always the same response"
he asked: "Is inflammation a good thing or a bad thing?" → generally good. "Then why do they tell you to [reduce it] when you sprain your ankle?" → because it's nonspecific — the full response is more than an ankle needs.
he asked: "Two hunter-gatherers 50,000 years ago — one sprains an ankle, one is bitten by a venomous snake. Whose inflammation matters most?" → the snake bite — without it they might die. The sprained ankle won't die, but inflammation makes the next weeks worse.
✎ his logic: cells don't know what's causing the injury, so they "go all out" every time. Today we know limiting inflammation after a sprain can speed healing.
he asked: "In a sprained ankle, is there any risk of bacteria?" (vs the snake) → immune cells still move in to clear dead cells and debris — or the venom.

6 · the inflammatory process & leukocytes

1vascular response — hemostasis (clotting) if bleeding · vasodilation: slower velocity but more blood flow
2↑ vascular permeability → fluid leaks out (exudate, edema); RBCs concentrate → redness, heat; pain, loss of function
3WBCs adhere to vessel walls, roll, then migrate into tissue (chemotaxis)
4leukocytes activate → phagocytosis and degranulation (secretion)
he asked: (sprained ankle, no bleeding) "What's the next step?" → vasodilation — more blood brings the things already in your blood that fight injury.
Kahoot: "What is the correct timeline of inflammatory events?" → vascular response → leukocyte adhesion & migration → activation & phagocytosis.
white blood cells (slide 50)
  • granulocytes — "degranulate" = secrete: neutrophils (phagocytosis, first), eosinophils (allergies, parasites, mildly phagocytic), basophils (allergies), mast cells (sentinels)
  • agranulocytes: monocytes → macrophages (inflammation + phagocytosis); T & B lymphocytes → adaptive (next week)
  • endothelial cells & platelets are "leukocyte-adjacent"
phagocytes (slides 51–54)
neutrophils — first responders, short-lived, become pus; clear debris, dead cells, bacteria
monocytes → macrophages — arrive ~24 h, replace neutrophils, live longer; orchestrate wound healing (debris, angiogenesis, cytokines, fibroblasts, collagen)
dendritic cells — link innate ↔ adaptive (T cells)
"You don't need to know the process of phagocytosis… just know phagocytosis is when a cell eats." (Five steps: recognition/adherence, engulfment, phagosome, fusion with lysosome, destruction.)

what activated complement does (slide 60)
  • opsonization — marks bacteria to be eaten
  • anaphylatoxic — smooth-muscle contraction, vasodilation, histamine release, ↑ permeability
  • chemotaxis — calls leukocytes
  • cell lysis — damages bacteria directly
he asked: "What process do they help with in phagocytosis?" → opsonization — "placing a marker on the bacteria."
he asked: "A patient with a cirrhotic liver — what would you expect in these systems?" → decreased complement, clotting and kinin proteins → more infections (and clotting problems).
also
  • acute-phase proteins (e.g. CRP) rise with inflammation, boost complement
  • kinin & clotting both activated by factor XII (Hageman)
  • the 3 systems amplify each other — must be tightly regulated: "effective, but too much is damaging"
Not tested: complement pathways (classical, lectin, alternative) — "I don't care how they're activated, just know they have to be activated" · clotting pathways (extrinsic/tissue factor, intrinsic/contact) · kinin details.

8 · cell-derived mediators & cytokines

Cells are activated through pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) that spot pathogens (PAMPs) and damaged cells (DAMPs) — "you don't need to know these terminologies."
endothelium & arachidonic acid (slides 66–67)
normally: NO & prostacyclin keep blood flowing, prevent clotting · damaged: exposed matrix → clotting + inflammation, signals immune cells.
membrane phospholipids → arachidonic acid
lipoxygenase → leukotrienes · cyclooxygenase (COX) → prostaglandins, thromboxane
blocked by: corticosteroids (phospholipid step) · aspirin, NSAIDs (COX)
he asked: "Anybody tell me the drug you take that decreases arachidonic acid [metabolites]?" → NSAIDs, aspirin — the hangover headache is a mini inflammatory response.
platelets · mast cells · basophils
  • platelets — fragments of megakaryocytes; activate → clot, release serotonin & growth factors
  • mast cells — most important cellular activator of inflammation; sentinels near vessels · basophils do the same in blood
  • degranulation (fast): histamine → dilates venules, ↑ permeability
  • then make: leukotrienes (slower, longer), prostaglandins (permeability, chemotaxis, pain), PAF (platelet aggregation)
he asked: "Histamine — what does that cause?" → allergy symptoms (more next week).

lethargy, depression, anxiety, malaise, no appetite, sleepiness, hyperalgesia · NSAIDs/Tylenol make you "perk up" — the infection isn't gone.

9 · acute vs chronic inflammation

acute — local (slide 80)

edema, pain, heat, erythema, loss of function · < 2 weeks ("some debate about 2–6")

exudate = cells and fluid that have seeped out of vessels:

serousearly/mild · watery, few proteins or WBCs
serosanguineousserous with some blood
fibrinoussevere/advanced · thick, fibrin
purulentpus · lots of WBCs (neutrophils), debris
sanguineoushemorrhagic · very bloody
(pseudo)membranouson mucous membrane, necrotic cells

ulceration: necrotic, eroded epithelial surface at the inflamed site.

acute — systemic (slide 81)
  • fever, lethargy — cytokines as endogenous pyrogens
  • leukocytosis > 10,000 — ANC is the better measure
  • acute-phase reactants from the liver: CRP, complement, fibrinogen
  • ESR ↑ — non-specific indicator of inflammation
he asked: "What are systemic manifestations of acute inflammation?" → big or long injury (car accident, not a sprain) → mediators reach levels that cause fever, sickness behavior, more WBCs, acute-phase proteins.
chronic (slide 82)
> 2 weeks · the acute response failed to clear the cause · pus, suppuration, incomplete healing · dense lymphocytes & macrophages · granuloma walls it off · classic example: tuberculosis
student asked: "What about autoimmune disease?" → autoimmunity causes cell injury, which triggers inflammation; many treatments overlap (next week).

10 · wound healing & what goes wrong

1hemostasis, angiogenesis, ingrowth of granulation (new) tissue — begins within 24 h
2fibroblasts migrate in and lay down extracellular matrix (the protein "scaffolding")
3maturation & remodeling of the fibrous tissue
phase (skin)whenwhat happens
hemostasisimmediateclot; fibrin mesh = scaffold; platelets degranulate, release growth factors
inflammatory1–2 daysneutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes move in · redness, pain
proliferative3–14 daysmacrophages clear debris, stimulate fibroblasts (collagen) & angiogenesis · granulation tissue (very red) · epithelialization (faster if kept moist) · myofibroblasts contract the wound
remodelingweeks – 2 yearsscar forms and remodels; scar is avascular · too much = keloid
primary intention — minimal tissue loss, edges close · faster
secondary intention — open wound (pressure ulcer), tissue lost · slower, more scarring, far more infection · sutures pull wide wounds together

repaired tissue ≈ 80% of original strength; replacement cells aren't the ones lost — except epithelium, bone marrow, liver.

dysfunctions (slides 91–92)
  • ischemia — "inflammation needs blood flow"; weak collagen
  • excess bleeding or fibrin, diabetes (small vessels, high glucose impairs macrophages), infection
  • nutrition: protein, vitamins A & C, zinc, iron… · medications · age
  • dehiscence — wound separates, highest risk days 5–12; obesity, abdominal wounds · evisceration can follow
  • contracture — excessive contraction, worst over joints
he asked: "Why do we make smaller incisions now?" → laparoscopic surgery — smaller incisions, fewer complications like dehiscence.
Kahoot: fibroblasts divide in which phase? → proliferative · put the phases in order → hemostasis/angiogenesis/granulation → fibroblasts & matrix → remodeling · keloid example: an ear piercing that "kept on healing."
