1 · trace the blood: valves & coronaries

"It's a lot much easier to know what happens when you know where blood is coming from and going next."

the loop (slides 4–5)
vena cava → RA → tricuspid → RV → pulmonic SL valve → pulmonary artery → lungs
pulmonary veins → LA → mitral → LV → aortic SL valve → aorta → body

right heart = unoxygenated blood → pulmonary circulation · left heart = oxygenated → systemic. Right AV valve = tricuspid, left AV valve = mitral, SL = semilunar (speaker notes, slide 5).

filling (slide 8)
AV valves open
semilunar valves closed
ejection
semilunar (aortic, pulmonic) open
AV (tricuspid, mitral) closed

a short moment with both sets closed as the ventricle starts to contract. "That is the ideal situation" — it comes back with valve disorders.

FYI — slides 6–7: ~64% of blood sits in the veins; intracardiac pressures (LV systolic ~130 vs RV ~24). "Don't need to memorize any numbers… just understand conceptually": the veins are a big reservoir (venoconstriction → more return → more CO in a healthy heart), and the RV is a low-pressure system that struggles to compensate when pressures rise (heart failure, lung disease).
key concept (slide 9)
coronary perfusion happens mainly in DIASTOLE — in systole the squeezing heart compresses its own vessels
artery (slide 9)main territory
LAD — left anterior descendinganterior LV + anterior septum ("most of the left ventricle")
circumflexlateral LV, parts of the LA — "wraps around towards the back"
RCARV; in right-dominant circulation, inferior LV + parts of the conduction system
✎ the LAD is "the widowmaker" — "it's supplying the biggest engine part of your heart." A block in a small downstream branch does far less damage than the whole vessel.
student asked: (a classmate's young relative died of an LAD heart attack) "What causes that? Is that genetic?" → probably a genetic predisposition to earlier atherosclerosis. "You have plaques in your body" — found from about age 3; most never rupture, but rupture is what causes a heart attack like that.
student asked: "Could you repeat what you said about the LAD?" → it supplies the left ventricle, the part doing most of the work; when the blood is cut off, "the muscle dies and does not contract anymore."
FYI — some people have slightly different coronary anatomy: "really beyond what you guys need."

2 · conduction, ECG & cardiac output

the SA node is the pacemaker (slide 10)
  • resting SA rate 60–100 bpm · innervated by the autonomic nervous system
  • SNS: NE → β₁ → ↑ HR, ↑ conduction, ↑ contractility
  • PNS: ACh → muscarinic → ↓ HR, ↓ AV nodal conduction
  • AV node: vagal receptors slow impulses — the normal pause between atrial and ventricular conduction lets the ventricles fill
she asked: (Kahoot) "Which receptor mediates sympathetic increases in heart rate and contractility?" → β₁ (answer from slide 10)

automaticity = spontaneous depolarizations · rhythmicity = regular generation of action potentials (slide 11)

cardiac output (slide 12 — "This is an equation!")
CO = HR × SV

average ≈ 5 L/min — for "a five foot eight, a hundred and eighty pound male"; adults vary. HR = beats/min · SV = volume per beat.

ejection fraction

slide 12 says 55–75%; she said "55 to 70 percent" twice — we use the spoken range. "The heart is never full yet" — some blood always stays behind.

PRELOAD = STRETCH (slide 15)
ventricular stretch at the end of diastole, set mostly by filling (end-diastolic volume)
determined by: venous return (filling pressure during diastole) + blood left after systole (end-systolic volume)
increased in: hypervolemia, valve regurgitation, heart failure (slide 18)
Frank–Starling (slide 16)
more fiber stretch → stronger contraction (actin & myosin at their best length) → more in = more out; venous return ↑ → CO ↑ — "up to a point"
stretch too far and the cross-bridges don't overlap enough → contraction gets worse. No hormones needed.
✎ "It's kind of like a rubber band. The further you stretch it out, the harder it bounces back."
student asked: (what makes more blood come back to stretch it) → "a big determinant of that is the venous return" — more venous constriction pushes blood forward, and a healthy heart pumps more.
AFTERLOAD = PRESSURE (slide 17)
the pressure the ventricle must overcome to eject · set by peripheral resistance (SVR) · must beat aortic (or pulmonic, on the right) pressure to open the valve
increased in: hypertension, vasoconstriction · ↑ afterload = ↑ cardiac workload (slide 18)
her numbers
BP 120/80 → the LV must make at least 121 mm Hg to open the aortic valve
BP 180 → at least 181 — "that should start to make sense why hypertension is rough on the heart"
✎ "Imagine on a windy day trying to push a door open when the wind's coming back at you."

slide 19's balloon: preload = air blown in (stretch) · contractility = how hard it flies · afterload = the knot it has to push air out through.

one more time (slide 20)
contractility = force of contraction · preload = STREEEETCHHHHH!!! · afterload = resistance to overcome · CO = HR × SV
she asked: (Kahoot) "Which equation correctly describes cardiac output?" → heart rate × stroke volume — multiply, don't add.
she asked: (Kahoot) "The best description of preload?" → "Stretch is the word."
she asked: (Kahoot) "What happens initially when venous return increases in a healthy heart?" → preload ↑ → stretches more → contracts harder → stroke volume ↑. "It's a little tricky" — she accepted the cardiac-output answer too.
she asked: (Kahoot) "Which change directly increases left ventricular afterload?" → ↑ systemic vascular resistance. ↓ volume usually lowers afterload; ↑ venous return raises preload (only indirectly afterload).
key concept (slide 21)
drug changes HR, preload, afterload or contractility → changes CO

4 · vessels, endothelium & resistance

vessel (slide 22)built for
elastic arteries — aorta, large branchesabsorb pressure → smoother, less swing between systolic and diastolic
muscular arteries — medium & smallsmooth muscle redirects flow (fight-or-flight: constrict to the gut, dilate to heart, brain, muscles)
arterioles (< 0.5 mm)mainly smooth muscle — major determinant of resistance
✎ arterioles are "teeny tiny… but there's so many of them" — a little constriction there makes a big change in pressure. That's where many BP drugs aim.
veins (slide 23)
thin walls · more connective tissue · larger diameter · more numerous · very little smooth muscle · valves for one-way flow (none in the CNS) · skeletal-muscle contraction pushes blood back to the heart — walking empties the leg veins
endothelium (slide 24)
the lining of the vessels — "sometimes considered a separate endocrine organ." Basic idea: substance transport · prevents excess clotting (antithrombogenesis) and breaks clots down (fibrinolysis) · immune function · tissue/vessel growth & wound healing · vasomotion (constrict/relax)
FYI — "you don't need to worry about that list, just basic ideas."
what changes flow (slide 25)
  • pressure gradient — blood flows from high → low pressure
  • diameter — larger = less resistance = more flow; smaller = more resistance = less flow
  • length — longer total length = more resistance
  • viscosity — dehydration → thicker blood → harder to flow
✎ "if you're watering with a big fat hose versus a tiny hose" — the small one needs more pressure to move the same water.
student asked: "How does length affect [resistance]?" → obesity: the body grows vessels to feed the extra tissue — "you have more miles of blood vessels," and that alone raises BP.
student asked: "So taller people would have higher BP?" → "not a correlation" as long as they're otherwise healthy — the heart compensates.
student asked: (does the body really grow new vessels?) → yes — angiogenesis is "the fancy name for new blood vessel growth."

5 · BP = CO × SVR and who regulates it

the master equation (slides 26–27)
BP = (HR × SV) × SVR

SVR = the total opposition the heart must overcome to pump into the systemic circulation. Most antihypertensive drugs "manipulate one or both sides of this equation" — next week.

side (slide 27 preview)lowered by
HRβ-blockers, non-DHP calcium channel blockers, central agonists
contractilityβ-blockers, non-DHP calcium channel blockers
circulating volume (preload)diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aldosterone antagonists
SVR (vasodilation)ACE inhibitors, ARBs, DHP calcium channel blockers, direct vasodilators, α₁ blockers
she asked: (Kahoot) "Arteriolar vasodilation — which variable does it most directly decrease?" → systemic vascular resistance
venous return (slide 31)

driven by the pressure gradient from systemic veins → RA; helped by skeletal muscle contraction, respiratory pressure changes (a deep breath pulls blood back), venous valves, sympathetic venoconstriction. Blood volume and gravity too — "if you put your legs up, you get more blood flow back."

regulator (slide 26)speedwhat it does
ANS — baroreceptor reflexseconds–minutes"pressure sensor" → reflex tachycardia or bradycardia to restore BP (slide 28)
RAAShours–daysangiotensin II constricts arterioles & veins (hours) · aldosterone retains water (days) (slide 29)
kidneysdays–weeksadjust volume
natriuretic peptides—released with volume overload (slide 30)
RAAS, one more time (00:26:35)
↓ BP or ↓ volume sensed by kidney → renin → angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → → angiotensin II
angiotensin II → potent vasoconstriction → ↑ SVR, ↑ BP
angiotensin II → adrenal → aldosterone → kidney holds Na⁺ + water → ↑ volume, ↑ BP
ANP & BNP (slide 30)
atrial and brain/B-type natriuretic peptide — "natri" = sodium · released when the heart is stretched · counteract volume overload: diuresis, dilate arterioles & veins, suppress RAAS
"not super strong, so we don't… manipulate them pharmacologically" — we use BNP as a marker: it climbs as heart failure worsens (diagnosis & severity — "we'll see BNP again… in like two weeks").
she asked: (Kahoot) "Which response helps counteract volume overload?" → release of natriuretic peptides. Angiotensin II and aldosterone are RAAS — that's when the kidney thinks there isn't enough volume.
factors affecting BP (slide 32)
↑ CO side: ↑ blood volume, skeletal-muscle pump, respiratory pump, venoconstriction → ↑ venous return → ↑ SV · ↓ parasympathetic + ↑ sympathetic/adrenal hormones → ↑ HR and SV
↑ SVR side: ↑ RBCs (polycythemia) → ↑ viscosity · ↑ body size (obesity) → ↑ total vessel length · vasoconstriction → ↓ vessel radius → ↑ mean arterial pressure
FYI — slide 33 (societal, community, personal and health-system factors): "Don't need to memorize these." Her point: hypertension is "super multifactorial" — even adverse childhood experiences raise the odds of high BP later.

6 · atherosclerosis: injury → plaque

arteriosclerosis = thickening and hardening of vessel walls (slide 36)
atherosclerosis = the most common form — lipid-laden macrophages build up in the arterial wall → a lesion called a plaque. A process, not one disease; can hit any vessel; leading cause of coronary artery and cerebrovascular disease.
✎ "Don't get hung up about like the steps" — sources number them differently. "Just understand it's a process."
1endothelial injury — smoking · hypertension (physical pressure) · diabetes (high glucose) · dyslipidemia (↑ LDL "bad," ↓ HDL "good") · autoimmunity · non-traditional: inflammation/thrombosis markers, infection, air pollution (slide 37)
2inflammation — inflamed cells stop making normal antithrombotic and vasodilating substances (slide 38)
3adhesion molecules bind macrophages & immune cells → macrophages damage more → free radicals oxidize LDL in the intima → oxidized LDL → even more adhesion molecules
4macrophages enter the intima and engulf oxidized LDL = foam cells → many together = fatty streak → more free radicals (slide 39)
5macrophage growth factors → smooth muscle proliferation → collagen migrates over the streak = fibrous plaque → may calcify, protrude, obstruct → symptoms
✎ adhesion molecules = "little sticky fingers that come out and start grabbing things from the blood that's flowing by."
✎ the fibrous plaque is "kind of like a scab over a wound."
it feeds itself
oxidized LDL → more adhesion molecules → more macrophages → more free radicals → more oxidized LDL: "a process that just keeps kind of self-perpetuating"
student asked: "Is it autoimmune?" → no — "it is chronic inflammation," but it isn't recognizing anything as foreign. Western diets and sedentary lifestyles drive much of it (vs the "blue zones": fresh food, lots of activity).

"Oftentimes, people have heart attacks without symptoms." Slide 41: plaques start in the first decade and stay clinically silent for years; growth is mainly lipid at first, then smooth muscle and collagen.

she asked: (Kahoot) "Which sequence best describes atherosclerotic plaque [formation]?" → endothelial injury → inflammation/adhesion → LDL oxidation → foam cells → fatty streak → fibrous plaque (answer from slides 37–39)

7 · rupture: where it happens decides

step 6: a complicated plaque (slide 40)
the plaque ruptures → underlying tissue exposed → platelet adhesion + clotting cascade → rapid thrombus → may suddenly occlude the vessel → ischemia and infarction
✎ "Think of like ripping a scab off of a wound… the body doing what it thinks it should do. It's like, oh, clot." Most plaques never rupture — "when they rupture, things can get real bad, real fast."
where it ruptures = the consequence
coronary artery → ACS / MI
cerebral artery → ischemic stroke
peripheral artery → acute limb ischemia
she asked: (Kahoot) "Why did that rupture cause an acute myocardial infarction?" → the rupture exposed tissue → platelets + clotting → a thrombus suddenly blocked the coronary artery → ischemia → infarction (answer from slide 40)
patho → pharm (slide 42)drug approach
↓ LDLstatins / other lipid-lowering drugs (today)
↓ platelet aggregationantiplatelet drugs
↓ thrombus formationanticoagulants, in appropriate conditions
restore flow through an acute clotthrombolytics ("clot buster") in selected emergencies

antiplatelets, anticoagulants and thrombolytics are "the last thing we cover before the exam" — not this lecture.

FYI — slides 43–44: Calgary Guide flowcharts + YouTube explainers. "They usually have way more depth than you need… if you look at this and it scares you… you can never look at it again."
high-yield review (slide 45)
  • chronic inflammatory process triggered by endothelial injury & dysfunction
  • LDL accumulation & oxidation → macrophages → foam cells → fatty streak → fibrous plaque
  • slow growth narrows the lumen — flow falls, especially when O₂ demand rises
  • rupture exposes thrombogenic material → platelets + clot
  • result: ischemia & infarction — MI, ischemic stroke

9 · who gets treated: prevention & lifestyle

primary prevention (slide 49)
prevent the first ASCVD event
lifestyle is foundational
statins first-line when indicated: ↑ LDL, diabetes, HTN, smoking, family history
decision weighs LDL level, diabetes, estimated CV risk, other risk factors
secondary prevention
prevent a recurrent event in established ASCVD — prior MI, atherosclerotic ischemic stroke, symptomatic PAD
high-intensity or maximally tolerated statin
more LDL-lowering drugs may be needed
after a heart attack: LDL < 70 (spoken)
abnormality (slide 50)why it matterstreatment emphasis
↑ LDLmajor driver of atherosclerosisstatins first-line (+ other LDL drugs)
↑ triglyceridesASCVD risk; severe → pancreatitislifestyle, secondary causes, TG-lowering drugs
↓ HDL↑ CV riskoverall risk; HDL-raising drugs haven't improved outcomes — exercise
therapeutic lifestyle changes (slide 51)
diet: ↓ saturated/trans fat; vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, unsaturated fats · exercise: ≥ 150 min/week moderate aerobic, when appropriate · weight management · smoking cessation (less endothelial injury) · limit alcohol, especially with high TG
✎ "Even tiny over time, five to ten millimeters of mercury… is going to make a difference" — when she started nursing, a systolic in the 130s was "good."
student asked: (when to start a statin vs try lifestyle) → depends on risk. After a heart attack, LDL below 70 — aggressive. A younger, healthier person with LDL 115 → maybe three months of lifestyle changes first, if willing.
student asked: "What about stopping?" → without other lifestyle changes, "you're just gonna bounce back to where your higher level was, and then your risk factors go back up."
student asked: "Young, healthy, it's genetic — should they be on a statin?" → "Once those numbers start going up," yes. "Some people… do everything right and the genetics is so far against you."
she asked: (Kahoot) "Which [treatment] strategy…" (question text not audible — reconstructed as the post-MI strategy) → secondary prevention: high-intensity or maximally tolerated statin + lifestyle (from slide 49)
✎ "I kind of feel like statins like we should all be on statins… such good drugs with so few side effects" — they stabilize plaque, and may lower Alzheimer's risk. (Her opinion.)

10 · statins — the gold standard

HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (slide 52)
atorvastatin (Lipitor) · rosuvastatin (Crestor) · simvastatin (Zocor) — "all the drugs have 'statin' in them"
block HMG-CoA reductase → ↓ liver cholesterol synthesis → ↑ liver LDL receptors → ↑ LDL pulled from the blood

"It not only decreases how much the liver is making, but it also causes the liver to suck up some of the stuff that's in the bloodstream."

  • substantial ↓ LDL · modest ↓ TG ("if your triglycerides are your only problem, statin is not the best choice")
  • ↓ risk of MI, ischemic stroke, other ASCVD events
  • gold standard for HLD (hyperlipidemia)
intensity (slide 53)LDL reduction
high≥ 50%
moderate30–49%
low< 30%
FYI — the doses in the table: "You don't need to know what doses are." Just: higher-intensity for higher levels or higher risk.
student asked: "Is that gold standard for HDL or HLD?" → HLD — hyperlipidemia. "We don't really have anything else for HDL."
RHABDOMYOLYSIS — "lots of questions for exams"
myalgia (aching muscles) can happen; rarely, muscle tissue breaks down → muscle contents flood the blood → kidney damage
sign: severe muscle pain + "Coca-Cola urine" (very dark brown — muscle pigment passing through the kidneys)
→ stop the statin, prompt evaluation; usually admitted for lots of fluids to flush toxins and protect the kidneys
she asked: (Kahoot) "A patient taking atorvastatin reports severe muscle pain and dark urine. What is…" → rhabdomyolysis (answer from slides 52, 54)
  • liver damage (rare) — LFTs before starting and often a couple of months after
  • slight ↑ diabetes risk — the CV benefits outweigh it
student asked: (do statins make diabetes harder to control?) → there's a slight increase in diabetes rates, but "all the other benefits are so amazing." She wasn't sure about blood-sugar control — "that is a good question."
nursing implications (slide 54)
  • baseline lipid profile + liver enzymes
  • monitor lipid response after starting or a dose change
  • ask about unexplained muscle pain, tenderness, weakness
  • severe muscle symptoms or dark urine → prompt evaluation for rhabdo
  • review drug interactions (FYI: "don't need to know" the CYP enzyme)
  • pregnancy plans — statins are generally discontinued during pregnancy
  • reinforce long-term adherence + lifestyle — "you're likely going to be on this medication forever"
student asked: "If you fix your lifestyle, you still can't come off the statin?" → "Very rare." Sometimes "genetics rules the day," and most adults don't make changes that drastic.

metabolic syndrome — "syndrome X" (slide 57)
a cluster that raises the risk of heart disease, stroke and diabetes mellitus · need 3 of these 5:
  • high triglycerides
  • low HDL
  • high blood sugar
  • high blood pressure
  • large waist circumference
FYI — the cutoffs (TG ≥ 150, HDL M < 40 / F < 50, fasting glucose ≥ 100, BP ≥ 130/85, waist M ≥ 40" / F ≥ 35"): "You don't need to memorize the numbers."
high-yield review (slide 58)
  • LDL is a major contributor → lowering it ↓ MI, ischemic stroke, ASCVD events
  • statins first-line: block HMG-CoA reductase → ↓ synthesis, ↑ LDL clearance
  • intensity matters: high ≥ 50%, moderate 30–49%
  • safety: muscle symptoms, interactions, liver injury · rhabdo rare but serious
  • add ezetimibe when a statin alone doesn't reach the goal
  • severe ↑ TG → pancreatitis risk → TG-specific treatment
  • risk-based: primary considers ASCVD risk; secondary = intensive LDL lowering
