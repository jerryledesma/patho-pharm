1 · how you get sick: C → I → V → P

terminology (slide 2)
host — the organism being infected ("all you guys right now are hosts")
agent — any factor (microorganism, chemical, energy) whose presence — or absence, in deficiency diseases — is essential for disease. The umbrella term.
pathogen — an organism that causes disease
colonization — living on you without damage = commensal (normal) flora; "if anything, they prevent disease"
infection — invasion of host tissues by an agent that causes disease or injury
he asked: "Example of a disease where the immune system attacks something the organism makes?" → whooping cough (pertussis) — the bacteria's toxin causes the cough. The vaccine is against the toxin, so you neutralize it while the bacteria are still there.
Ccommunicability — ability to spread from one person to others. You can't "randomly" get a disease — "it has to come from somewhere." measles & pertussis spread very easily (measles lingers in a room up to 8–10 h); HIV does not (needs sexual contact)
Iinfectivity — ability to invade and multiply in the host; proportion of exposed people who become infected. a few cold particles may not get into cells · COVID in vaccinated vs unvaccinated
Vvirulence — ability to cause severe disease; proportion of the sick who become severely ill or die. cold = low · rabies = high ("99.9% going to die" once symptomatic)
Ppathogenicity — ability to cause disease; success depends on C, I and V together. COVID "six years ago": high C, I and V → high pathogenicity

portal of entry: direct contact, inhalation, ingestion, animal/insect bites — "where it's entering is also very important."

✎ rabies: the virus travels slowly from a finger bite to the brain — that's why the vaccine given after the bite can still work.
student asked: can you spread COVID before symptoms? → yes — some diseases spread while asymptomatic. Rabies is the opposite: the animal has to be symptomatic.
virulence factors (slide 4)

immunogenicity = ability to induce an immune response — "the other term I want you to know from here."

FYI only — "you don't need to memorize this": toxigenicity (toxins), adhesion factors (stick/enter), evasive/immunosuppressive factors.
antigen vs antibody (slide 5)
antigen
"any substance that the immune system recognizes" — e.g., a toxin, a microbe's surface, a transplanted organ

antibody — Y-shaped proteins in the blood made in response to an antigen. Immune cells also carry antibody-like receptors on their membranes.

FYI only — epitopes (the part of an antigen an antibody attaches to): "getting too granular."

2 · the pathogens: prions → parasites

"Most of this you guys can read by yourself" — know these at a broad level (what each is, the big example, gram-negative vs positive, exo- vs endotoxins).
prions (slide 6) — infectious proteins
no genome · abnormally shaped (misfolded) versions of your own proteins · make normal proteins misfold → clump → cell death · degenerative CNS disease, e.g. mad cow disease
his story: 1990s slaughterhouses — infected brain matter on meat; infectivity is rare and it takes a decade or two to show.
viruses (slides 7–8)
protein coat around a nucleic-acid core (DNA or RNA; retroviruses e.g. HIV) · most common affliction of humans · must infect a host cell: attachment → penetration → uncoating → replication → assembly → release · usually self-limiting (cold), exceptions (HIV) · spread by aerosol, infected blood, sexual contact, vectors (mosquitoes)
cell injury: lysis · stop DNA/RNA/protein synthesis · lysosome disruption · fused giant cells · altered antigens (immune attack) · cancerous transformation · secondary bacterial infection
fungi (slide 12)
large, thick walls (polysaccharides, not peptidoglycan) · yeasts (spheres) & molds (hyphae) · antibiotics will not kill fungi · mycoses: superficial (skin, mucosa, mild; tineas — capitis, pedis, cruris) vs deep (serious; don't develop with an intact immune system)
parasites (slide 13)
protozoa to worms (malaria, amoebic dysentery, giardiasis; helminths) · not common in the US · rarely person-to-person — vectors (ticks, mosquitoes…) or contaminated food/water
bacteria (slides 9–10)
prokaryotes — no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles · cell wall · make biofilms · aerobic/anaerobic
Gram stain: purple = positive, pink = negative · gram-positive is more receptive to antibiotics
bacteremia (presence) vs septicemia (infection) — usually gram-negative
he asked: "Does the extra layer make gram-negative bacteria more or less destructive?" → more — it evades the immune system and antibiotics. "These are like the armored tanks of the bacterial world… anytime you have gram-negative, it's not a good sign."
student asked: which color is which? → "pink is negative, purple is positive."
EXOtoxins = "exit"
proteins purposely released during growth, constantly · enzymes that damage membranes, block protein synthesis · e.g. diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis · antitoxins = antibodies against them
ENDOtoxins = "inside"
LPS of gram-negative bacteria · released only when bacteria are attacked / lysed — "kill those immune cells that just killed them" · pyrogenic (fever) · ↑ vascular permeability
how pathogens fight back (slide 11)
  • bacteria: thick capsules block phagocytosis · toxins destroy neutrophils · proliferate too fast
  • viruses: hide in cells · spread cell to cell · hard to kill without killing the host cell · antigenic variation (flu)

3 · diagnosis, disease course & infection control

local / specific
diarrhea (GI — "Taco Bell lettuce"), rash, convulsions, hemorrhage, pneumonia
systemic / nonspecific
fever, myalgia, headache, lethargy (sickness behavior)
he asked: "Why would they be considered systemic, not local?" → student: the headache has nothing to do with the brain → right — vasodilation and whole-body inflammation (neck, shoulders); "your brain has no sensory" receptors. Hangover headache = same idea.
diagnosis (slide 14)
  • site of infection? port of entry (e.g., an STI → sexual encounter)
  • culture — grow the organism outside the host (throat swab for strep); rapid antigen tests aren't perfect, so cultures are still sent
  • serology — measures serum antibodies; titer rises after exposure · IgM rises and falls in the acute phase · IgG stays elevated after
  • detection of antigens, genomic sequences, metabolites

IgM/IgG serology bullets are slide-only (not discussed).

incubation
exposure → first symptoms; pathogens colonizing
prodromal
first, often mild symptoms; still multiplying
acute
full-blown; immune & inflammatory responses; pathogens invading
convalescent → resolution
immune system clears it; symptoms decline
  • subclinical disease — below the clinical threshold: "scratchy throat" one day, fine the next; little to no signs/symptoms
  • past the critical threshold → death
  • chronic — kept at bay but not cleared: TB is walled off in the lung — "not 100% secure"
  • the acute phase can last 7–14 days — that's when adaptive immunity kicks in
infection control (slide 16) — "stop it before it starts"
environmental: waste disposal, water treatment, food safety, vector control · hygiene: hand washing, face masks, social distancing, quarantine, isolation — the nurse's job, so the disease course never starts.
"natural immunity": you always run the risk of dying — flu kills tens of thousands a year; risk rises over 40.

5 · making 10¹² receptors: rearrangement & selection

the diversity problem (slide 28)
> 10¹² different antigen receptors · only 26,000 protein-coding genes
he asked: "How many proteins our DNA actually makes?" → the point: far too few genes for 10¹² receptors (slide 28: 26,000 genes).
he asked: "Is DNA very protected? Any DNA damage — what happens to that cell?" → it dies. "Immune system cells are different" — they purposely cut and rejoin their DNA.

somatic variation (hypermutation): a few gene segments are recombined — and cut imprecisely on purpose — to generate diversity. "This is more kind of a conceptual thing."

FYI only — V/D/J segment counts and the math (2.64 × 10⁶): "I don't expect you guys to memorize."
where they're made & selected (slide 29)
all produced in the bone marrow (immature)
B cells mature/selected in the bone marrow — "You need to know that."
T cells mature/selected in the thymus (in front of your throat)
objective: quality, not quantity
positive selection
binds nothing → told to die · "useless… waste of resources"
negative selection
binds self too strongly → told to die · "that's how you prevent autoimmune diseases"
positive + negative selection = TOLERANCE
Kahoot: "this is also called tolerance."
he asked: "Do you think every time that cell makes an antibody, it's going to be for pathogens?" → no — "hitting the lottery." Useless cell → should it stay alive? → no, waste of resources.
he asked: "That immune cell really likes to bind the neurons. Good or bad?" → bad — autoimmunity → negative selection.
✎ the thymus shrinks with age — the theory for why immunity wanes and autoimmunity rises. "A large part of your childhood is developing this repertoire." Thymus regions: "we don't need to go into detail."
then: stored in a lymph node (slides 30–32)
each finished (naive) cell travels to a lymph node and sits there — usually just one cell per antigen. Lymphoid organs = the "bases/forts."
he asked: "Why is it just one? Why not 1,000 copies?" → 10¹² kinds — you'd run out of space.
he asked: "When that cell divides on infection, what do we call that?" → clonal proliferation — one cell copies itself over and over (mitosis) so enough can leave the node.

6 · APCs, MHC II & clonal proliferation · antibody functions

antigen-presenting cells (slide 33) — "scouts in the field"
macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells (technically innate) take in the invader, process it, and put a piece on the cell-surface MHC molecule → signal T cells.
"Just remember, it's MHC class II." In humans: HLA antigens.
1APC picks up antigen at the infection site, presents it on MHC II
2travels to the closest lymph node
3goes T cell to T cell — "hey, do you match?" Only T cells whose receptors fit respond
4match → activation & clonal proliferation → leave the lymph node
he asked: "Clinical example of when T cells and B cells divide — something you do to patients?" → palpating lymph nodes. Swollen = cells proliferating: good (immune system working) and bad (active infection). Closest node responds — infection in the foot → node behind the knee.
who does what (slide 35)
  • T helper cells = coordinators — "don't technically get their hands dirty"; release cytokines to direct the others
  • APC + naive T helper → activated T helper · APC + naive cytotoxic T → activated cytotoxic T (with helper help)
  • B cells + activated T helpers → plasma cells → antibodies
  • effector cells (the "soldiers"): cytotoxic T cells and macrophages, directed by T helpers
  • afterward: memory T/B cells → secondary response
antibody functions (slide 38)
direct (antibody alone): neutralization (viruses, toxins) · agglutination (clumps cells — bacteria can't move) · precipitation (soluble antigens)
indirect (other components): inflammation · phagocytosis · complement

Slides 37/47: B cells become plasma cells that secrete antibodies (he said B cells "stay in the lymph node" and release antibodies — the slide wording is used here).

7 · the step-by-step ("know this part") · primary vs secondary · vaccines

✎ "Now, this is very important. If you guys just know this part, you're going to be good to go." · "Students always get this wrong" — step 1 happens before infection.
1develop a population of T and B cells specific for antigens (10¹²), not against self, stored in lymph tissue — before infection
2infection (e.g., strep throat — someone sneezes in your face)
3innate system fights it (could be the final step)
4if bad, with lots of inflammation → APCs present antigen on MHC II
5APCs travel to the closest lymph tissue
6if the right T cell is there, it binds the APC's MHC II/antigen
7clonal proliferation of T cells; activation of the same B cells
8T cells leave lymph tissue; B cells → plasma cells churning out antibodies
9T cells find the infection site (cytokines, chemokines, inflammation)
10T cells recruit macrophages, cytotoxic T cells, other effectors to kill it
11memory T/B cells form; antibody production continues
he asked: "What is the very first thing that needs to happen to fight infection?" → develop a population of T and B cells — before the infection.
he asked: "Why aren't infants constantly dying of infection?" → breast milk antibodies (infant stomachs aren't very acidic yet); parents get vaccinated. No T/B cells at all = the "bubble boy" (SCID).
primary (slide 51)
antigen on MHC II → T helpers → B cells proliferate → plasma cells → antibodies; memory B cells made · antibody levels rise over 2–3 weeks · vaccination produces a primary response
secondary (slide 52)
second/later exposure · memory B cells respond immediately · levels rise within days · booster shots cause a secondary response so levels are high before the disease is met

vaccines (slides 37, 51): weakened (attenuated) or dead pathogens, or recombinant viral protein · long-lasting immunity · CDC schedules · expensive to develop · "reluctance to vaccinate but complications rare."

he asked: after vaccination, "when they get released, do they attack anything?" → no — no active infection; they eventually die but leave a bigger population → secondary response. "This does wane a little bit" → boosters.
student asked: are these cells stored in the bone marrow or the lymph node? → B cells are selected in the bone marrow, T cells in the thymus — both head to the lymph node.
student asked: why does vaccine efficacy differ? → incubation & mutation: measles (~1 week–10 days, rarely mutates) → vaccine prevents illness · COVID (3–4 days) / flu (quick) → trials measured hospitalization and death, not "not getting sick."
student asked: what about sepsis? → it's your immune system going nuts — pro-inflammatory cytokines, BP drop. COVID deaths too: "immune system going haywire" → corticosteroids tamp it down.
he asked: "If someone is vaccinated against pertussis, can they still spread it?" → yes — the vaccine targets the toxin, not the bacteria, so we need high vaccination rates (Northwest outbreak). Tetanus is also anti-toxin.
Vaccination deck (V-slides 2–13): not taught in the recording — he said "I'll post it later." From the slides; testable.
mRNA (COVID) vaccine step-by-step (V-slides 2–12)
1 T/B cells specific for Spike2 already in lymph nodes (selected in thymus / bone marrow) · 2 mRNA vaccine given · 3 innate system attacks the mRNA — mRNA is very immunogenic (non-immunogenic vaccines add an adjuvant); muscle cells take up the mRNA and make Spike2 · 4 APCs (dendritic cells, macrophages) present Spike2 on MHC II · 5 to the closest node — shoulder shot → armpit · 6 matching T cell binds · 7 clonal proliferation; B cells activated · 8 T cells leave; plasma cells make anti-Spike2 antibodies · 9 they find no infection → activated cells expire (apoptosis), antibody production falls · 10 no effector cells recruited · 11 memory T/B cells + continued antibodies — 10–14 days
side effects 24–48 h (V-slide 5)
lethargy · myalgia · fever · headache — from the innate inflammatory/immune response to the vaccine
exposed ≥ 2 weeks after vaccination (V-slide 13)
1 antibodies bind Spike2 → effector cells/complement destroy the virus before it infects cells · 2 huge viral load gets in → no symptoms yet, may still spread it; memory T cells proliferate, recruit effectors and B cells · 3 fought off quickly (likely asymptomatic), contagious briefly (unvaccinated: up to 2 weeks); minor symptoms possible but recovers rather than hospitalized or dead

type I — "allergic reactions" (slides 57–58)
allergen binds antigen-specific IgE on mast cells. First exposure → processing and priming of mast cells; later exposure → degranulation of histamine. Histamine is the main chemical behind the symptoms.
GI diarrhea, vomiting · skin urticaria (hives), pruritus · mucosa conjunctivitis, rhinitis · airway bronchoconstriction, mucus, edema · vascular angioedema, hypotension
genetic predisposition · tests: food challenges, skin tests, lab tests
anaphylaxis (slides 59–60)
systemic, can be life-threatening: ACh, kinins, leukotrienes, prostaglandins → bronchoconstriction → hypoxia · histamine (+ the same) → vasodilation → shock
he asked: "What's anaphylaxis?" → the airway closes so you can't breathe, plus too much vasodilation → shock. "A very serious medical emergency."
he asked: "If you've never been stung by a bee, can you be allergic to a bee sting?" → no — "you can only have an immune reaction to something that your immune system has seen." = the sensitizing event.
he asked: after the first sting, when the T/B cells leave the lymph node, will they see the venom? → no — it takes 7–14 days; the allergen is long gone. Stung again a month later → possible reaction. More stings → more risk.
he asked: "What is the problem with this APC [peanut]?" → it found a T cell specific for peanuts that should have been selected out — the gut normally builds tolerance.
✎ peanuts: avoiding them in year 1 was "horrible, horrible advice." Now: don't forgo peanuts even with a family history — watch for S&S, diverse diet, mom can eat peanut butter while breastfeeding. Allergy shots (weekly grass antigens) build tolerance.
he asked: kids with dogs or farm time develop fewer allergies — why? → constant, diverse exposure lets positive/negative selection happen correctly.

9 · H1 antagonists (antihistamines)

✎ "They don't do anything with the actual immune system" — mast cells still release histamine; it just can't bind the blocked receptor.
mechanism & effects (slide 61)
block histamine at H1 receptors
peripheral: ↓ localized flushing, ↓ itching and pain
CNS (therapeutic dose): CNS depression · 2nd generation: negligible
overdose: CNS stimulation, convulsions — very young children especially sensitive
uses: allergy · motion sickness · insomnia
1st generation
highly sedating — diphenhydramine (Benadryl). "Histamine [is] responsible for keeping you awake. If you block those receptors, you go to sleep."
2nd generation (slide 65)
much less sedation · cross the BBB poorly · low affinity for CNS H1 · largely no anticholinergic actions · OTC: cetirizine (Zyrtec), fexofenadine (Allegra), loratadine (Claritin)
adverse effects (slide 62)
sedation (less with 2nd/3rd gen) · nonsedative CNS: dizziness, fatigue, poor coordination, confusion · GI: nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, constipation — give with food · anticholinergic: weak atropine-like effects · severe respiratory depression · severe local tissue injury (vessel constriction)
his line: you'd "really, really have to take a lot" for respiratory depression
interactions & toxicity (slide 63)
CNS depressants add up · large margin of safety — acute toxicity "very, very rare," but widely available · toxic signs: dilated pupils, flushed face, hyperpyrexia, tachycardia, dry mouth, urinary retention · children: CNS excitation · extreme: coma, CV collapse, death
FYI only (not discussed): poisoning treatment — no antidote; charcoal, cathartic, IV benzodiazepines for convulsions, ice packs for hyperthermia.
high-risk patients (slide 64)
contraindicated3rd trimester (uterine contraction) · nursing mothers (sedation, ↓ milk) · newborns (seizures, arrhythmias, death)
cautionyoung children (respiratory depression, seizures, paradoxical hyperactivity) · older adults (confusion, sedation, constipation, blurred vision, urinary retention, falls, possible dementia link) · conditions worsened by muscarinic blockade

10 · types II, III & IV

type II — tissue-specific, antibody-mediated (IgG/IgM) · slides 66–68
antibodies bind a specific cell or tissue:
· cell destroyed by antibody + complement (e.g., a red blood cell)
· cell destroyed by phagocytosis
· tissue destroyed by complement + neutrophil granules
· antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC)
· target-cell malfunction — antibodies block a receptor "like antagonists"
myasthenia gravis — the classic type II
antibodies block ACh receptors on muscle → ACh can't bind → flaccid muscle, paralysis — droopy eyelid (ptosis)
treatment (slide 68):
· AChE inhibitors like pyridostigmine — more ACh by stopping its breakdown
· corticosteroids — ↓ the immune overreaction
· thymectomy — too many cells got through selection
· plasma exchange & IVIG in myasthenic crisis — clean antibodies out of the blood
type III — immune complex (slides 69–70)
free antigen + antibody → circulating complexes ("giant roaming balls") → deposit in vessel walls/tissues → complement → enzymes → damage · not organ specific
antigens: penicillin, DNA… (exo- and endogenous)
serum sickness (prototype) — foreign serum protein, e.g. antivenom → blood vessels, joints, kidneys: fever, enlarged lymph nodes, rash, pain, arthritis
systemic: autoimmune vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, serum sickness · SLE (DNA)
local: Arthus reaction — local inflammation/necrosis
he asked: a child gets a local reaction (Arthus) at a vaccine site — why? → student: already exposed → B cells ready → antibodies meet the concentrated antigen at the injection site → complexes clog it up.
type IV — cell-mediated (slide 71)
T / Tc cells, not antibodies · sensitized T cells attack the antigen · direct cytotoxicity · 24–72 hours
examples: TB skin test · poison ivy, metals · graft rejection · multiple sclerosis
treated with immunosuppressives (glucocorticoids/corticosteroids)
he asked: "Have you guys ever had a TB skin test?" → if you've had TB, that was the sensitizing event; T cells travel to that patch of skin, activate and proliferate — that takes time (slide: 24–72 h).

11 · autoimmunity & alloimmunity

autoimmunity — the 3 causes he wants (slide 73)
"know that it's either genetic predisposition, breakdown of tolerance or molecular mimicry"
· genetic predisposition — more common in women
· breakdown of tolerance — self-antigens get recognized as foreign (thymus didn't select properly)
· molecular mimicry — e.g., MS and flu, Epstein-Barr, coronavirus
environmental: swine flu vaccine · self-antigens not normally seen by the immune system
student asked: "I'm allergic to shellfish, but my mother ate plenty while pregnant — how?" → an APC probably presented shellfish protein during a gut infection to a T cell that fit only ~80% — "it's not a perfect system".
FYI only — the MS/flu and rheumatoid-arthritis (knee injury releases avascular proteins) theories: "more than you need to know."
systemic lupus erythematosus (slide 74)
most common, complex, serious autoimmune disorder · autoantibodies to nucleic acids, histones, ribonucleoproteins → circulating immune complexes = type III · more common in women · chronic multisystem: arthritis, vasculitis & rash, renal disease, hematologic (esp. anemia), CV disease · remissions and exacerbations · hydroxychloroquine, glucocorticoids, NSAIDs
alloimmunity × 3 (slides 76–81)
1 · blood transfusion (type II): A & B carbohydrate antigens — A, B, O = neither, AB = both · O = universal donor · AB = universal recipient · wrong type → transfusion reaction
2 · fetus (type II): Rh-negative mother, Rh-positive father/child → after birth she is Rh sensitized → erythroblastosis fetalis / hemolytic disease of the newborn · give Rho(D) immune globulin (RhoGAM, Rhophylac, MICRhoGAM) IM
3 · transplant: MHC/HLA are targets · crossmatch = donor + recipient blood mixed (lysis = incompatible)
✎ Rh: the first baby is fine — no sensitizing event yet. Birth is the sensitizing event; the problem is the second Rh-positive baby.

he asked: bone marrow transplant — why can the donor attack the recipient? → bone marrow is where immune cells come from → graft-versus-host disease (type IV, slide 81).
