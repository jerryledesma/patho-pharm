1 · what heart failure is · causes

Heart failure = a clinical syndrome in which structural or functional cardiac abnormalities impair ventricular filling and/or ejection (slide 4).

impaired filling and/or ejection → inadequate cardiac output + / or elevated filling pressures
  • from impaired contraction (systolic dysfunction), impaired relaxation/compliance (diastolic dysfunction), or both
  • common manifestations: fatigue, exercise intolerance, dyspnea, fluid congestion
  • chronic and progressive, or acute decompensation — "most of the time… this is a long, chronic process"
  • the common final pathway of many CV diseases: CAD, hypertension, valvular disease
Kahoot: which finding best describes heart failure? → (slide 4) impaired ventricular filling and/or ejection → ↓ CO and/or ↑ filling pressures
cause (slide 5)how it leads to HF
CAD / MIischemia & injury impair contractility; infarction = loss of functioning myocardium
hypertensionchronic pressure overload → ↑ workload → hypertrophy & stiffness
diabetes & obesityvascular dysfunction, metabolic stress, inflammation, adverse remodeling
valvular diseasechronic pressure or volume overload (if not corrected)
cardiomyopathiesdirect abnormalities of myocardial structure & function
othercongenital heart disease, arrhythmias, some pulmonary/systemic disorders
✎ her pearl: you don't need a big MI — many partial blockages mean "you could be losing a heart cell here, a heart cell there," and the damage accumulates. An MI just kills "a huge chunk of cells… at once."
Kahoot: what most directly contributes to HF after an MI? → loss of contractile myocardium — "Good… the more cells you lose," the more contractile force you lose.
Kahoot: how can long-standing hypertension contribute to HF? → (slide 5) pressure overload → hypertrophy & stiffness

2 · systolic vs diastolic · HFrEF vs HFpEF

systolic dysfunction — can't squeeze (slide 6)
ventricle can't generate enough force in systole · MI = irreversible loss of cardiomyocytes · chronic ischemia
ventricle may dilate → more blood left behind after each beat (↑ end-systolic volume) → ↓ EF & SV → ↓ CO → triggers compensation
her image: a balloon blown up over and over — "big, floppy," the elasticity worn out
diastolic dysfunction — can't relax / fill (slide 7)
relaxation delayed or compliance ↓ → stiff ventricle needs higher pressure to fill · HTN & aging are common · hypertrophy, fibrosis, ischemia
SV may fall even with a preserved EF · high LV filling pressure → pulmonary congestion
"We need both — to contract hard, but also then relax to allow blood to come back in."
EF is a percentage ✎
Diastolic HF pumps out a normal % of a smaller volume — "the percentage is normal, but the overall cardiac output is less." A normal EF ≠ normal stroke volume or normal filling pressures.
slide 8normalsystolic (HFrEF)diastolic (HFpEF)
fillingnormalenlarged, often more volumestiff, less blood
EF~55–70% (pumps ~60%)≤ 40%≥ 50%, SV may be ↓
Kahoot: HF symptoms + EF 35% → which category? → HFrEF — under 40% is reduced ejection fraction.
Kahoot: HF symptoms, stiff LV, EF 60% → most likely? → HFpEF — "All right, good… We are in the normal range."
Kahoot: why can SV be reduced even when EF is preserved? → starting out with less blood — "Good."

FYI only — HFmrEF: "I'm not too much worried about that one." HFimpEF: e.g., after treatment or cardiac rehab — "we still have not gotten rid of the heart failure."
✎ both HFrEF and HFpEF can cause ↓ effective CO, ↑ filling pressures and congestion; EF alone doesn't determine severity (slide 10). Why tell them apart? Because the treatment is different — HFrEF has the proven drugs.

3 · compensation: SNS & RAAS

↓ CO → the body tries to keep BP and perfusion up. Initially protective, maladaptive when chronic (slide 12). "The body thinks that it is trying to save itself… good in the short term, but very detrimental to the heart in the long term."

response (slide 12)short-term benefitlong-term consequence
SNS activation↑ HR, contractility, vasoconstriction↑ workload, O₂ demand, afterload
RAAS activation↑ BP and circulating volumefluid retention, congestion, remodeling
↑ preloadmay ↑ SV (Frank–Starling)excessive filling pressures, congestion
hypertrophymore force for the workload↑ O₂ demand, stiffness, impaired function
SNS (slide 13)
↓ CO → ↓ arterial pressure → baroreceptors → ↑ sympathetic activity
  • β₁ — ↑ HR & contractility
  • α₁ — arteriolar vasoconstriction → ↑ SVR & BP
  • venoconstriction — ↑ venous return & preload
  • renal β₁ — renin release → RAAS

Chronic: persistent tachycardia ↑ O₂ demand; vasoconstriction ↑ afterload (harder to eject); catecholamines → cell injury, dysrhythmias, adverse remodeling.

✎ her pearl: catecholamines, angiotensin II and aldosterone are "kind of like poison to the heart cells" — the biggest things keeping HF getting worse, so they're our drug targets. Patho → Pharm: beta blockers.
Kahoot: why does SNS activity initially increase in HF? → compensation — keep BP up and tissues perfused
Kahoot: which chronic SNS effect makes HF worse? → ↑ myocardial O₂ demand and afterload
RAAS (slide 14)
↓ CO → ↓ renal perfusion → renin → angiotensin II
angiotensin II
vasoconstriction → ↑ SVR → ↑ afterload
aldosterone
Na⁺ & water retention → ↑ volume → ↑ preload

Chronic RAAS → congestion (lungs & periphery) · ↑ workload (preload + afterload) · fibrosis (↓ compliance) · remodeling (dilation, progressive decline).

Kahoot: angiotensin II raises cardiac workload primarily by causing… → vasoconstriction → ↑ afterload; it also stimulates aldosterone.
Kahoot: aldosterone contributes to congestion by increasing… → sodium and water retention

Patho → Pharm: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs and MRAs each interrupt a different part of this pathway.

FYI only — aldosterone "maybe like 20 times higher" in HF — "don't quote me."

4 · preload, hypertrophy & remodeling

increased preload (slide 15)
  • poor ejection leaves more blood behind; RAAS Na⁺/water retention ↑ volume & venous return
  • healthy heart: more stretch → more force (Frank–Starling)
  • failing ventricle: that ability is limited → extra volume raises filling pressures without improving CO
key concept
In HF, excessive preload produces more congestion rather than more forward flow. Patho → Pharm: diuretics ↓ volume & filling pressures.
Kahoot: why can additional preload eventually worsen HF? → (slide 15) it raises filling pressures and congestion without raising output
remodeling (slide 17)

Progressive change in ventricular size, shape, structure and function from chronic injury and stress. Drivers: persistent SNS (catecholamine injury) · Ang II & aldosterone (hypertrophy, fibrosis) · chronic pressure/volume overload · myocyte injury & loss · fibrosis (stiffness).

remodeling → worse systolic/diastolic function · ↑ O₂ demand · dysrhythmias → progressive HF
✎ Patho → Pharm: guideline-directed drugs do more than relieve symptoms — they can slow (partly reverse, esp. in HFrEF) remodeling. She put it more cautiously: "We can't stop it completely, we definitely can't reverse it, but we can slow it down by a lot."
eccentric hypertrophy (slide 16)
volume overload → chamber enlarges — "big and floppy"
e.g., valvular regurgitation, some dilated cardiomyopathies
→ ↑ wall stress, systolic dysfunction
concentric hypertrophy
pressure overload → wall thickens, chamber smaller — "really thick-walled"
e.g., chronic HTN, aortic stenosis ("like pumping against some really high blood pressure")
→ stiffness, diastolic dysfunction
Kahoot: which pattern is most associated with chronic pressure overload? → concentric — "Oh, good"; eccentric goes with volume overload.
high-yield review (slide 18)
↓ CO → SNS + RAAS · SNS ↑ HR, contractility, vasoconstriction, renin · RAAS ↑ afterload (Ang II) and preload (aldosterone) · preload helps then congests · pressure/volume overload → hypertrophy · chronic neurohormones → fibrosis, myocyte injury, remodeling · HF drugs target the mechanisms, not just the symptoms

5 · forward vs backward · left- vs right-sided HF

forward failure — ↓ output (slide 20)
inadequate flow to tissues & organs → fatigue, weakness, exercise intolerance, ↓ renal perfusion
backward congestion
high filling pressures pushed back into pulmonary or systemic veins → dyspnea, crackles, peripheral edema, JVD

Either or both — in HFrEF or HFpEF.

Kahoot: which symptom is most directly linked to reduced forward output? → fatigue / weakness — not enough blood getting to the tissues.
left-sided HF → the lungs (slides 21–22)
backward: ↑ LV filling pressure → ↑ left atrial & pulmonary venous pressure → fluid into lung interstitium & alveoli
→ dyspnea on exertion · orthopnea · paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea · cough · cyanosis · basilar crackles
forward: ↓ LV output → fatigue, oliguria, ↑ HR (compensation), faint pulses, restlessness, confusion, anxiety ("signs of the brain not getting enough") · ↓ renal perfusion → more RAAS
✎ her pearl: ask "how many pillows do you use to sleep at night?" — once HF gets bad, "you lay flat and you drown in your lungs," so some sleep almost sitting up (orthopnea). PND is "the same process, it just happens more slowly" — fall asleep, wake up with lungs full of fluid.
usual order ✎
The left ventricle fails first (most work) → blood backs into the lungs → the right ventricle, used to "very little work," now pumps against high pressure → left-sided HF leads to right-sided HF. Most clients have both.
right-sided HF → the body (slides 23–24)
causes: left-sided HF (most common) · pulmonary hypertension & chronic lung disease · RV infarction
backward: ↑ right atrial & systemic venous pressure → JVD · peripheral (dependent) edema · hepatomegaly · ascites · splenomegaly · anorexia
forward: less blood to the lungs → less LV filling → ↓ CO — the same forward signs as left HF
  • hepatomegaly — gut blood goes to the liver first, so it engorges early
  • ascites — fluid in the peritoneal cavity
  • anorexia = loss of appetite (not anorexia nervosa) — "you don't feel like eating when your GI tract is all fluid"
  • edema in dependent areas — legs, or the back if lying down
she asked: "Why do I even care about this big old vein?" → heart failure — the jugular drains into the right atrium; ↑ pressure → JVD when sitting up. "You shouldn't" see it.
student asked: what makes the right side fail? → having to pump harder — usually the left side backs blood into the lungs; rarely pulmonary hypertension alone.
Kahoot: which finding most strongly suggests systemic venous congestion? → (slide 23) JVD / peripheral edema — "Good."

6 · BNP · stages & classes

B-type natriuretic peptide (slide 25)
released by ventricular cardiomyocytes in response to ↑ wall stress / stretch — "hey, there's too much fluid in here"
→ Na⁺ & water excretion + vasodilation — the opposite of RAAS, but "not nearly as strong"
BNP / NT-proBNP support HF in the right clinical context — not specific, not diagnostic alone
higher with renal dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, age · obesity may suppress it · low values make HF less likely
✎ her pearl: trend it — if a client's BNP "is like three times what it was the last time," something new is going on; as treatment works, it falls.
Kahoot: what triggers BNP release in HF? → (slide 25) ↑ ventricular wall stress / stretch
Kahoot: which statement about BNP is most accurate? → (slides 25, 27) supports HF in context but isn't specific or diagnostic alone
ACC/AHA stages — progression
A at risk — risk factors, no symptoms or structural disease (HTN, diabetes, CAD, obesity, family hx)
B pre-HF — no symptoms, but structural disease, ↑ filling pressures or biomarkers (prior MI, LVH, ↓ EF)
C symptomatic HF (current or previous symptoms)
D advanced — severe symptoms despite optimized therapy (mechanical support, transplant, palliative)
generally only move forward
NYHA classes — symptoms
I no limitation, no symptoms
II slight limitation — symptoms with ordinary activity
III marked limitation — symptoms with less-than-ordinary activity
IV symptoms at rest
can improve or worsen with treatment

Stage A is newer — "let's not wait until heart failure actually happens… address all these risk factors." Stages C–D are "a big, huge continuum."

Kahoot: which classification describes HF progression from risk to advanced HF? → (slide 26) ACC/AHA stages
three different questions
EF category = which dysfunction · ACC/AHA stage = how far along · NYHA class = how limited right now

Kahoot: which list contains the four foundational HFrEF classes? → ARNI/ACEi/ARB · β-blocker · MRA · SGLT2i
loop diuretics — furosemide (Lasix) · slide 30
block Na⁺/Cl⁻ reabsorption in the thick ascending limb → ↑ Na⁺ & water excretion → ↓ volume & filling pressures → relieve pulmonary congestion, edema, dyspnea
thiazide-type may be added for persistent congestion (different part of the nephron) — close monitoring; both waste K⁺
adverse: hypovolemia, hypotension, dehydration · hypokalemia ("the biggest electrolyte that we are worried about") · renal changes
monitor: weight, fluid status, BP, electrolytes, renal function
✎ her pearl: "almost every heart failure patient is on them," and clients feel much better — but loops don't treat the underlying disease and don't prolong life.
Kahoot: what distinguishes loop diuretics from foundational HFrEF therapy? → they don't treat the underlying process — feel better without living longer.
ACE inhibitors & ARBs (slide 31)
lisinopril (Zestril/Prinivil) ↓ Ang II formation · valsartan (Diovan), losartan (Cozaar) block AT₁
↓ vasoconstriction (↓ afterload) · ↓ aldosterone (less Na⁺/water) · ↓ remodeling · better HFrEF outcomes
safety: hypotension, hyperkalemia, renal changes · ACEi: cough, angioedema · contraindicated in pregnancy · monitor BP, K⁺, renal
ARNI — sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto) · slide 32
valsartan blocks AT₁ · sacubitril inhibits neprilysin (breaks down natriuretic peptides) → more natriuresis & vasodilation · ↓ CV death & HF hospitalization in HFrEF
safety: hypotension, hyperkalemia, renal dysfunction, angioedema · never with an ACE inhibitor · 36-hour washout switching ACEi ↔ ARNI · avoid with angioedema history
"better on paper," so many are switched — cost holds some back
Kahoot: sacubitril primarily acts by… → inhibiting neprilysin so natriuretic peptides aren't broken down
Kahoot: how long must an ACE inhibitor be stopped before starting an ARNI? → 36 hours
she asked: why isn't sacubitril given alone — and why with an ARB, not an ACEi? → it ↑ RAAS a lot, so it needs a RAAS blocker; and it slows bradykinin breakdown, so with an ACEi → angioedema risk.
FYI only — that sacubitril reasoning: "You don't need to know this. It's just mine."

8 · MRAs · beta blockers · SGLT2 inhibitors · HFrEF vs HFpEF

MRAs (slide 33)
spironolactone (Aldactone), eplerenone (Inspra) · block mineralocorticoid (aldosterone) receptors → ↓ Na⁺ retention & K⁺ excretion · ↓ aldosterone fibrosis & remodeling · ↓ mortality & HF hospitalizations in HFrEF — more than their modest diuresis
adverse: hyperkalemia (esp. with RAAS inhibitors or renal dysfunction) · worsening renal function · spironolactone: gynecomastia & other endocrine effects
key: disease-modifying, "not simply another way to increase urine output"
β-blockers in HF (slide 34)
metoprolol succinate ER (Toprol-XL), carvedilol (Coreg) · block chronic SNS → ↓ HR & O₂ demand · ↓ catecholamine injury & remodeling · ↑ long-term function & survival
may initially ↓ contractility & CO → start low, titrate up when stable · doses "a lot lower" than for HTN · monitor bradycardia, hypotension, worsening congestion, fatigue · may defer start/increase during significant acute decompensation
SGLT2 inhibitors (slide 35)
dapagliflozin (Farxiga), empagliflozin (Jardiance) · block SGLT2 in the proximal tubule (made for diabetes) → glucose & Na⁺ out in urine → modest natriuresis & diuresis
↓ HF hospitalizations · with or without diabetes · across the HF spectrum
safety: genital fungal infections (sugar in the urine) · volume depletion, hypotension · withhold in major illness, prolonged fasting, surgery (ketoacidosis risk)
✎ the β-blocker paradox: "you would think… the heart is not pumping very well," so why slow it? Because a low dose blocks the "poisonous effects of the excess" catecholamines — it prolongs life.
Kahoot: which electrolyte abnormality is a major concern with spironolactone? → (slide 33) hyperkalemia
Kahoot: why can beta blockers improve long-term outcomes in stable HFrEF? → they block harmful chronic sympathetic stimulation
Kahoot: which statement about dapagliflozin and empagliflozin is correct? → they improve HF outcomes even without diabetes — "Yes."

SGLT2 inhibitors were "kind of found accidentally" to be great for HF. More in the diabetes lecture.

FYI only — the SGLT2 mechanism diagram (slide 36): "You don't need to know any of those things about this."

9 · digoxin · putting the drugs together

digoxin (Lanoxin) · slide 38
↑ contractility (positive inotrope) while ↓ HR (slows AV conduction) — "one medication that will increase contractility, but decreases heart rate"
may improve symptoms / ↓ HF hospitalizations in selected HFrEF · no survival benefit · not one of the four pillars
narrow therapeutic index
toxicity ✎
dysrhythmias · nausea/vomiting · anorexia · confusion · visual disturbances
↑ risk: hypokalemia (loop diuretics!) · renal dysfunction (accumulation) · interacting drugs
Kahoot: a client on furosemide and digoxin develops hypokalemia — the concern? → (slides 38–39) digoxin toxicity
FYI only — how it works (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺): "Don't worry about how it's working."
nursing (slide 39 + her rule)
1take the pulse first — "you do not take this medication if the heart rate is below 60" (and follow ordered hold parameters)
2monitor K⁺, Mg²⁺, renal function
3watch for new dysrhythmias, GI, neuro, visual changes; review interactions
4consistent dosing — never double up a missed dose ("they can really bottom out your heart rate")
Holding it loses some symptom relief, but "it's not going to be detrimental like not giving some of those other medications."
Slide 39 says to follow ordered hold parameters "rather than treating HR below 60 as a universal rule"; she taught < 60 = hold. Use her rule for her exam.
