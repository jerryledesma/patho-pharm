1 · how air gets in: diaphragm, recoil, compliance & the pleura

what the lungs do (slide 2)

primary function: exchange gases between the environmental air and the blood. "The lungs and the heart, they work in concert to do the same job."

other functions: immunity · regulate vasoconstrictor substances (break down bradykinin and serotonin; convert angiotensin I → II; produce heparin) · blood reservoir (the lungs "dish it out" when the periphery needs extra blood)

he asked: "Why do you think the lungs care about things that constrict blood vessels?" → the lungs are full of blood vessels, vital for gas exchange, so they break vasoconstrictors down.
VENTILATION
mechanical movement of air between atmosphere and lungs
aka respiratory rate
DIFFUSION
transfer of gases
alveolus ("still outside") → blood
PERFUSION
blood flow
O₂ carried to the rest of the body
  • respiration (scientific) = production of energy, O₂ in, CO₂ out; he uses it loosely for "breathing things in"
  • minute volume = ventilatory rate × volume of air per breath
  • lobes: 3 on the right, 2 on the left (the heart tilts left)
  • steps (slide 3): ventilation → external gas exchange (load O₂, unload CO₂) → gas transport → internal gas exchange → cellular respiration
he asked: "Have you guys heard of lumen before?" → the inside of any structure — the lumen of an alveolus, of a blood vessel. "We're kind of like a hollow tube": the alveolar lumen is still the outside environment.
airways (slide 4)
conducting airways
upper: nasopharynx, oropharynx · lower: larynx, trachea, bronchi/bronchioles
move air in, warm and humidify it, trap inhaled particles (dust, bacteria; cilia)
respiratory airways
bronchioles + alveoli (ducts/sacs) — thin enough for gas exchange
"north of 95%" of gas exchange happens in the alveoli
FYI only — "I'm not going to ask a question on a test" whether the larynx is upper or lower: "for this lecture it is the boundary."
he asked: "What's one way that we can reduce resistance in a closed space?" → make it bigger. Anything flowing through a tube meets friction; a wider airway = less airway resistance. "This will come into play… with asthma and COPD."
★ the diaphragm
the main inspiratory muscle. Contracts and flattens (U-shape pulls down) → negative pressure "like a vacuum" → air in. Breathing out is mostly passive.
FYI only — "I don't expect you guys to memorize the muscles": accessory inhalation (external intercostals, scalene, sternocleidomastoid) and exhalation (internal intercostals, abdominals) muscles — used when you're "being chased by a bear."
elastic recoil (elastance) — the lungs return to resting state after inspiration: "you wind up the spring," relax the diaphragm, and "the spring just goes right back," pushing the air out.
compliance — ability to stretch (the stiffness of the spring). Low compliance requires more force. Chest-wall restriction: deformation, immobilization (broken ribs, a chest wrap) and/or obesity.
he asked: "If compliance is low, meaning that's very stiff, what does that mean for your breathing?" → more energy from your diaphragm to expand the chest.
he asked: "What if you have really easy compliance?" → student: less work to breathe → "Sure… but will that compliant spring spring back?" → the air gets trapped; you can't exchange air properly.
he asked: "What happens to somebody if… it doesn't have recoil?" → can't blow off enough CO₂, so you're not getting enough O₂ down → hypoxic.
student asked: (about exhaling with accessory muscles) → exhaling is normally little work, but these patients have problems with the exhale.

hyperventilation is "something else": acidic blood (Dr. Lalani's lecture) → you hyperventilate to blow off CO₂; panic also does it.

the pleura (slides 7–8)
a smooth, two-layered serous membrane lubricated by fluid from serum
parietal pleura = lines the chest cavity · visceral pleura = attaches to the lung
between them: the pleural space (cavity) with pleural fluid (~30 mL) — all synonyms
surface tension (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) links lung to chest wall and keeps the lungs "popped open"
he asked: why are lungs as flimsy as "the grocery bags from… Dominick's or Jewel"? → they must be very thin for gas diffusion — so they need the fluid-filled sac to stay open (the water-balloon model).
puncture: chest side or lung side → pressures equalize → flimsy lung collapses → less O₂ diffuses
student asked: does the pleural cavity just fill up with more [fluid] to protect the lung? → a chest-side puncture fills it with air; a hole on the lung side leaks inhaled air into it — "your lungs are going to collapse either way."
FYI only — "you don't need to know all information here": the pressure numbers (atmospheric & intra-alveolar 760, intrapleural 756 mm Hg; 760 = 760 after a puncture).
Kahoot: which serous membrane lines the thoracic cavity? → parietal (the visceral lines the lung) · T/F: during inhalation the diaphragm contracts and flattens → true · more or less energy to inflate the lungs when compliance is decreased? → more ("if your lungs are very stiff… it's going to take more energy")

2 · airways, mucus & alveoli (CF, surfactant, two arteries)

he asked: "What is the point of branching this many times?" → more surface area for gas exchange. Zone names (slide 11): "I don't really care if you guys know that."
airway epithelium (slide 12)
ciliated epithelial cells secrete a watery saline layer (water + electrolytes) · goblet cells secrete only mucus
mucus traps inhaled particles; cilia move it toward the pharynx
he asked: why secrete both mucus and watery fluid — "what could be the problem if it's too thick of a mucus?" → you clog your pipes — like pouring bacon fat down the drain.

2Na⁺ follows the negative charge
3water follows the NaCl → thins the mucus to "the perfect ratio"
he asked: "Chloride is a negative charge. What follows?… And then what follows sodium?" → sodium, then water.
cystic fibrosis (slide 13)
CFTR is defective → no Cl⁻ secreted → Na⁺ and water never follow → very thick mucus → blocked airways → hypoxia + secondary bacterial infections ("guess who loves [mucus]? Bacteria").
FYI only — the CF drug "cocktail" that raises airway fluid, and the other organs CF affects (e.g. the GI tract).
he asked: "What is this red stuff right here [around the bronchiole]?" → smooth muscle — it contracts and constricts the airways.
type I alveolar cells
very thin · line 95% of the alveoli · cannot regenerate
type II alveolar cells
~5%, larger · make surfactant · can divide → replace type I or II
he asked: "Why can't type I cells divide?" → they're purposely kept very thin and small so gas diffusion can happen; type II cells have the machinery to divide.
he asked: "What did I tell you about the pleural cavity and the fluid that's in it?" → surface tension keeps the lungs open.
he asked: "Do we really want surface tension inside the lumen of the alveoli?" → no — they'd collapse.
surfactant
"a fancy word for soap" — breaks the surface tension inside the alveoli → ↑ compliance, alveoli stay open
bugs stand on water because they don't break its surface tension · slide 14 also lists "deep breaths"

also in the alveoli: macrophages. Alveolocapillary membrane = the shared alveolar and capillary walls — gas exchange occurs across it.

bronchial artery
the main artery that supplies the lung tissue itself with oxygen — "that's the only one"
pulmonary artery
carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs to get oxygenated (the whole RV output) · does not feed the lung tissue
he asked: "What is the official [definition] of artery?" → away from the heart. "99.9% of arteries carry what kind of blood?" → oxygenated — the pulmonary artery is the exception; the pulmonary veins are the veins that carry oxygenated blood (to the LA).
pulmonary vessels (slide 16)
pulmonary circulation has a lower pressure than systemic · vessels narrow when their smooth muscle contracts · constriction/dilation respond mainly to local conditions · most important cause of pulmonary artery constriction: low alveolar PO₂ · acidemia also constricts
Kahoot: the lung tissue gets its blood/oxygen from? → the bronchial artery · gas exchange occurs in the… → (answer not audible; slide 14: across the alveolocapillary membrane) · which is true of the pulmonary system? → (not stated; slides 2 & 4: the larynx is the upper/lower boundary, the left lung has 2 lobes)

3 · V/Q matching & diffusion: why patients get hypoxic

airways and blood vessels both constrict and dilate — "they have to work together" so good blood meets good air.

he asked: this alveolus is damaged — "why is this capillary constricting?… where is the blood going?" → otherwise it would pass the dead alveolus, get no O₂ and go to the body — wasted blood. The capillary says "I am not wasting my time here" → constricts and sends blood to working alveoli.
↓ airflow to a region → ↓ PO₂ → pulmonary vasoconstriction → blood diverted to healthy areas
↓ blood flow to a region → ↑ PO₂, ↓ PCO₂ → bronchoconstriction → air diverted to healthy areas
FYI only — the local signals behind it: "I'm not gonna expect you guys to know that."
V/Q ratio (slide 18)
ventilation ÷ perfusion — "Q, for some reason, stands for perfusion"
normal ≈ 0.8 (4 L/min air ÷ 5 L/min blood)
"I don't expect you guys to remember the numbers" of the mismatch examples
he asked: "When you have a mismatch… are they gonna get the right amount of oxygen, too much, or low oxygen?" → low — hypoxic.

he asked: a large area of damaged alveoli — "what are all these capillaries gonna do?" → constrict. "What does that mean for your heart and your lungs?" → ↑ blood pressure in the lungs → work on the heart → pulmonary hypertension.
student asked: do a low V/Q and a high V/Q both lead to constriction and higher blood pressure? → yes.
he asked: "What happens when you lift weights? What happens to your muscles?" → they get bigger — same for the smooth muscle of vessels and airways: constant constricting → hypertrophy → narrower lumen, less flow, ↑ pressure → pulmonary hypertension. "You don't want that kind of adaptation."
diffusion — the concept "you don't need to remember this law… it tells you conceptually what's happening"
diffusion ∝ Δ concentration × surface area × permeability ÷ distance
  • bigger gradient (high → low) = more diffusion — breathing replenishes alveolar O₂
  • more surface area = more diffusion
  • thicker membrane (↓ permeability) = less
  • longer distance = less ("the closer something is, the more diffusion")
he asked: "Where do you have more oxygen, out in front of your face or down in your lungs?" → in front of your face — breathing in replenishes O₂ and keeps the gradient big.
he asked: why is a normal alveolus puffed "like a cloud"? → surface area.

4 · carrying O₂ & CO₂: SaO₂, CO poisoning, the Bohr shift (CADET)

CO₂ transport (slide 21)
  • CO₂ diffuses from cells → systemic capillaries → venous blood perfuses the pulmonary capillaries → diffuses into alveoli → removed by ventilation
  • venous blood (slide 21 chart): ~60% bicarbonate · ~30% carbaminohemoglobin (CO₂ bound to Hgb) · ~10% dissolved (arterial 90 / 5 / 5)
  • dissolved CO₂ = PaCO₂ · slide text: 75% in the RBC, 25% in plasma
carbonic anhydrase same equation as Acid–Base
CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺

exhale → remove CO₂ → less carbonic acid → blood pH ↑.

Kahoot: T/F: PaCO₂ and pH are directly correlated → false — "when CO₂ goes up, what happens to pH? It goes down" (Dr. Lalani's lecture).
oxygen (slide 22)
  • O₂ "does not like to be dissolved" — only 1.5% dissolved; the rest rides on hemoglobin (4 O₂ per Hgb)
  • SaO₂ = % of available Hgb bound to O₂ · measured by pulse oximeter · normal 95–100%
  • "Remember: Hgb is a protein that can change how it folds"
he asked: "For you that owns a fish, what do you have to do to make sure they stay alive?" → a bubbler — oxygen won't stay dissolved in water, so we need Hgb. CO₂ dissolves easily (a can of Coke).
he asked: "Can anybody tell me a time where you would force more [oxygen] to be carried in the blood?" → carbon monoxide poisoning — CO binds where O₂ should, "irreversibly," so you give oxygen to force dissolved O₂ into the blood. (spoken only, not on the slides)
he asked: "What are some things [that] can change [a protein]?" → temperature, pH, partial pressures.
he asked: "Where does oxygen bind the hemoglobin?… what does oxygen need to do when it gets to my finger?" → binds in the lungs, unbinds in the tissues.
lungs → shift LEFT
alkalosis (CO₂ leaving), hypocapnia, cooler inhaled air → Hgb "loves" O₂ (↑ affinity, binds)
slide: e.g. hyperventilation
periphery → shift RIGHT
metabolism → acid, more CO₂, warmer → Hgb "hates" O₂ (↓ affinity, kicks it off to the cells)
slide: acidosis, hypercapnia (inadequate respiration)
he asked: "Why are we saying alkalosis in the lungs?" → CO₂ is leaving the blood — slightly basic, less CO₂. Why lower temperature? → the air you breathe in is cooler than 98.6°.
he asked: "What happens in your periphery?" → more CO₂, slightly acidic, warmer → O₂ kicked off.
CADET, face Right! "increase in CO₂, acid, DPG, exercise, temperature… all going to shift" right
  • CCO₂ ↑
  • AAcid ↑ (pH ↓)
  • D2,3-DPG ↑
  • EExercise
  • TTemperature ↑

Bohr effect = the lower pH at peripheral tissues releases O₂. The S-shaped curve: "blood enters your lungs, left shift; leaves your lungs, right shift."

FYI only — the other labels on the O₂ curve ("I don't expect you guys to know"); 2,3-DPG at altitude / "live high, train low" (he self-corrected; not on the slides).

5 · terms, control of breathing (FYI) & lung volumes

Slides 25–26: "You guys can read it by yourself" — read quickly in class; testable definitions.
respiratory failure — the lungs can't get enough oxygen into the blood
cyanosis — bluish skin and mucous membranes from increasing desaturated (reduced) Hgb
clubbing — bulbous enlargement of the end of a digit, usually with chronic disease (chronic hypoxia)
hypercapnia / hypocapnia — increased / decreased CO₂ in the blood
hypoxemia — reduced oxygenation of arterial blood from respiratory alterations · PO₂ < 60 mm Hg
hypoxia — reduced oxygenation of cells in tissues, caused by alterations of other systems
Numbers: slide 25 defines hypercapnia as PCO₂ > 50 mm Hg. For reading ABGs these notes use Acid–Base (Dr. Lalani): PaCO₂ 35–45, so > 45 = high.
dyspnea — breathing discomfort, chronic or acute: air hunger, breathlessness, shortness of breath, preoccupation with breathing
DOE — dyspnea on exertion ("go up some stairs")
orthopnea — dyspnea when lying flat · heart failure (the LV can't handle the blood arriving from the lungs)
PND (paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea) — nighttime gasping for air, coughing
hemoptysis / hematemesis — coughing / vomiting of blood
✎ clubbing: "they still don't know why clubbing happens." His car mechanic — late 50s, overweight, sleep apnea, "his fingers are huge" — "get tested, and get a CPAP."
FYI only — the respiratory centers (slide 27): "this is just for FYI." Medulla (control center; output via vagus & phrenic nerves) · pons · central chemoreceptors sense PCO₂ and pH in the CSF (↑ breathing when PCO₂ ↑ or pH ↓; in long-term COPD they become insensitive to small PaCO₂ changes) · peripheral chemoreceptors sense arterial PO₂ (↑ breathing when PO₂ < 60) · irritant, stretch and J receptors. "The main way [the body knows we're hypoxic] is measuring CO₂." Stretch receptors stop a deep breath from over-inflating the lungs.

6 · PFTs: FVC, FEV1 & the ratio (obstructive vs restrictive)

FVC
forced vital capacity (L): all the air you can forcefully exhale after a full breath in
"the gas tank" — a 15-gallon tank · how much air the lungs hold
FEV1
forced expiratory volume in 1 second of the FVC test
how fast you empty the tank · tests ventilation through the conducting pathways
PEF
peak expiratory flow (L/min): maximal flow rate during expiration
degree of obstruction in the airways
normals (slides 31–33)
FVC ≥ 80% of predicted · FEV1 ≥ 80% of predicted · FEV1/FVC ≥ 0.70
predicted from gender, age, height, race/ethnicity · uses: diagnose chronic lung disease, monitor it over time, gauge severity
he asked: "In that first second, should you be able to blow out most of your air?" → yes — FEV1 is very close to FVC.
he asked: "What do you think FEV1 is testing… ventilation, diffusion, perfusion?" → ventilation. "Which lung part… is crucial for ventilation?" → the conducting pathways.
he asked: why measure expiration, not inspiration — "easier to… breathe in air or to expire air?" → breathe in: the diaphragm can overpower some airway resistance, but no muscle pushes air out — so obstruction shows on the exhale.
restrictive
lungs don't hold enough air · FVC ↓ and FEV1 ↓ about the same → ratio unchanged
balloon blown only halfway → less pressure behind the air → less out in the first second
obstructive
lungs can't conduct enough air · FEV1 ↓ more than FVC → ratio < 0.70
breathe through one nostril (~1.5 s vs ~1 s) → never fill the lungs → FVC falls too
he asked: why does FEV1 fall in restrictive disease and FVC fall in obstructive? → the balloon and the one-nostril demos. "A balloon… blown full vs half — same air out in the first second?" → no.
he asked: "If both FEV1 and FVC go down… how do you tell the difference?" → the FEV1/FVC ratio.
his ratio math (student asked: "can you say that again?")
normal: FEV1 0.7 ÷ FVC 1.0 = 0.7 ("just at the border of normal")
restrictive: FVC falls to 0.7, FEV1 to 0.49 → 0.49 ÷ 0.7 = 0.7 — ratio unchanged
obstructive: FVC 0.7, FEV1 0.3 → 0.3 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 0.43 — FEV1 fell at a sharper rate
student asked: do all obstructive diseases decrease the ratio? → yes — "all of [the obstructive] diseases have a decrease in that FEV1/FVC ratio."

8 · the drugs: categories & mechanisms (ICS, SABA/LABA, SAMA/LAMA, combos)

✎ "I don't expect you guys to know the proper names… If you guys just know the categories and the mechanisms, you guys are going to be good." Names below are examples only.
anti-inflammatory agents
work on the immune side — glucocorticoids (ICS, oral, IV) · mast-cell stabilizer · leukotriene modifier
bronchodilators
let more air in — β₂ agonists (SABA, LABA) · anticholinergics (SAMA, LAMA — COPD) · methylxanthines
glucocorticoids — the foundation (slide 48)
foundation of asthma & COPD therapy · daily, long-term · inhalation preferred = ICS (PO and IV exist) — e.g. beclomethasone (ICS), prednisone (oral)
MOA — suppress inflammation: ↓ release of mediators (histamine, prostaglandins) · ↓ inflammatory-cell activity (eosinophils, leukocytes) · ↓ edema of the airway mucosa
SE (systemic): adrenal suppression, slowed growth, bone loss, hyperglycemia
he asked: why is inhaled the preferred route? → delivered right to the place having the problem.
he asked: "If you're properly taking inhaled corticosteroids, should you be getting a big systemic effect?" → no — only if overused. (Adrenal suppression: "your adrenal glands will be like, oh… we won't make any more.")
other anti-inflammatories (slide 49)
mast-cell stabilizer (cromolyn, inhaled) — ↓ degranulation · not preferred; glucocorticoids work better
leukotriene modifier (zafirlukast, oral) — ↓ eosinophil recruitment · ↓ bronchoconstriction, edema, mucus
inhalers (slide 50)
advantages: drug goes directly to the site · systemic effects minimized · rapid relief of acute attacks
types: MDI (metered-dose, hand-held) · Respimat (very fine mist) · dry-powder (breath-activated) · nebulizer (machine, mist)
he asked: "Beta-2 adrenergic agonists. What does that mean?" → they activate β₂ receptors → dilate the bronchioles.

★ the step-up rule
attacks / SABA use 2 or more times a week → step up
"It was more than two. Now the rule is if they have two or more, you need to step up" — and he inked "2 or more" on slide 57. The printed slides still say > 2 days/week.
he asked: "Why is this important to step up their treatment?" → prevent smooth-muscle hyperreactivity and hypertrophy — "the only way… is by making sure they are not getting their attacks."
1SABA PRN (intermittent)
2low-dose ICS, every day (alt: cromolyn, LTRA, nedocromil, theophylline)
3low-dose ICS + LABA or medium-dose ICS
4medium-dose ICS + LABA
5high-dose ICS + LABA (consider omalizumab for allergies)
6high-dose ICS + LABA + oral corticosteroid

  • step up if needed — first check adherence, environmental control and comorbid conditions
  • step down if well controlled at least 3 months · one step at a time
  • quick relief: SABA up to 3 treatments at 20-minute intervals; a short course of oral systemic corticosteroids may be needed
  • consult an asthma specialist at step 4+ (consider at step 3)

acute asthma attack (slide 58) — "you guys can read that"
chronic: long-term control drugs (ICS most important) + quick-relief SABA PRN
acute (SABA not working → ER): oxygen for hypoxemia · a systemic glucocorticoid for airway inflammation · a nebulized SABA to open the airways · ICS

slide 59 (not discussed) — goals: prevent chronic symptoms · SABA ≤ 2 days/week · normal lung function and activity · fewer ED visits · always combine a LABA with an ICS · systemic glucocorticoids are a last resort.

COPD treatment (slides 60–64)
diagnosis: FEV1/FVC < 0.70 + symptoms · stages I mild → IV very severe
goals: ↓ symptoms · ↓ risk and mortality by preventing progression and exacerbations
exacerbation = acute worsening beyond day-to-day variation that changes the medication: ↑ dyspnea, sputum, cough (± wheeze, chest discomfort, cold symptoms) · usually viral and/or bacterial infection
stepwise, and only forward: "for COPD, you're never going to go backwards" · LABA or LAMA · long-term ICS for severe symptoms or when LABAs fail — always with a LABA · PDE4 inhibitors ↓ inflammation, cough
stable vs exacerbation: same drugs, different formulations · LABAs for stable; SABA + inhaled anticholinergic for exacerbations · antibiotics if S&S of infection (sputum = "a breeding ground for bacteria") · O₂ to sats > 88–92%

10 · restrictive disorders: aspiration → ARDS

"Restrictive diseases reduce the capacity for your lungs to hold air. That is it." ↓ compliance/volume → ↓ diffusion of O₂ into the blood → hypoxemia (slide 66). "They all work the same way."

aspiration (slide 67)
fluid and solid particles pass into the lungs · when the swallow and cough reflex are impaired · elderly especially — keep the HOB elevated!
S&S: coughing, choking, vomiting, dyspnea, wheezing
Tx: supplemental O₂ · mechanical ventilation · corticosteroids (inflammation) · antibiotics (infection) · prevent aspiration pneumonia
he asked: "How does aspiration reduce air?" → your friend makes you laugh while you drink → liquid in the alveoli takes the air's place.
atelectasis (slide 68)
collapse of lung tissue (not the entire lung) · types: contraction (fibrotic/scar) · resorption (an airway blocked) · compression (fluid, tumor pressing)
frequent after surgery and in CHF · S&S: fever, cough, dyspnea, leukocytosis
Tx/prevention: cough & deep-breathing exercises — after thoracic surgery "you're going to give them a[n incentive] spirometer"
pneumothorax (slide 69)
air or gas in the pleural space → destroys its negative pressure; air takes up space · S&S: pleural pain, tachypnea, dyspnea, absent or ↓ breath sounds
primary: spontaneous, healthy people, usually men 20–40 · secondary: chest trauma
open: air moves in and out — the lung partly reinflates
tension: a flap snaps shut on the exhale → air builds with every breath → shifts the heart and the other lung — "tension is always going to be worse than open"
pleural effusion (slide 70)
fluid in the pleural space — takes up room like a pneumothorax · "just kind of know what kind of fluid there is":
hydrothorax serous fluid · empyema pus · chylothorax lymph · hemothorax blood · urinothorax urine
transudative
↑ hydrostatic or ↓ plasma oncotic pressure forces fluid in · e.g. hypertension
exudative
high protein in the pleural sac pulls fluid in · from inflammation
he asked: "That high protein is going to do what? To fluid?" → pull it in (exudative).
pneumonia (slide 71)
infection of the lower respiratory tract (fungi, bacteria, viruses, parasites) · most common: Streptococcus pneumoniae · inflammation fills alveoli with fluid
risks: old age, immunocompromise, immobilization, malnutrition, cardiac or liver disease, nursing-home residence
community-acquired vs hospital-acquired (≥ 48 h after admission) · Tx: antibiotics (monitor resistance)
pulmonary edema (slide 72) — excess water in the lungs
① valve disease / CAD / LV dysfunction → ↑ left atrial pressure → ↑ pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure
② capillary endothelial injury → ↑ permeability, surfactant disrupted → fluid and proteins into the alveoli
③ blocked lymphatics → fluid can't be removed from the interstitium
"it doesn't necessarily have to be from infection"
ARDS (slides 73–74) — light: back "at the end of the semester when you talk about COVID"
acute lung inflammation + diffuse alveolocapillary injury → edema in the alveoli; neutrophils release mediators, enzymes, ROS · complicates > 30% of ICU admissions
causes: sepsis, burns, aspiration, bypass, overdose, radiation · defined by bilateral infiltrates on CXR, low PaO₂, no left atrial hypertension
course: dyspnea, hypoxemia → hyperventilation, respiratory alkalosis → ↓ perfusion, metabolic acidosis → ↑ work of breathing, hypoventilation → hypercapnia, respiratory acidosis → failure
Tx: oxygenation & ventilation, prevent infection, prone position; in COVID, glucocorticoids to tamp down the immune system ("goes berserk")

11 · TB: latent vs active, TST, RIPE

✎ "I want you guys to know about TB." TB "eats your lungs" — giant holes — so it's a restrictive disease.
the organism (slides 75–76)
Mycobacterium tuberculosis — gram-positive acid-fast bacillus; likes dark, humid, aerobic places · very slow-dividing, hard to kill · world's foremost single-agent infectious killer
spread by airborne droplets (sneeze, cough) · lungs or disseminated
taken up by macrophages & neutrophils but resists them → granuloma (Ghon focus): macrophages, T cells, inactive bacteria — the immune system walls it off
3–6 weeks to develop a positive TB test · can harbor bacilli for life — latent can always become active
latent (dormant)
walled off · no symptoms · not contagious · can break out "at any time"
active
symptomatic, contagious · necrosis & cavitation · new transmission or reactivation · death without Tx
S&S (slide 77)
starts like a bad cold · low-grade fever · night sweats · fatigue, malaise · frequent deep productive cough · chest pain · weight loss · immunocompromised → atypical
diagnosis & high risk (slides 79–80)
test: clinical signs · a + skin or blood test · high risk
definitive: CXR, sputum culture, NAA test
high risk: TB contacts · prisoners, homeless · immigrants within 5 y · kids < 4 · nursing-home residents · DM, chronic renal failure, leukemia
TB skin test — Mantoux / PPD / TST (slide 81)
intradermal antigen from M. tuberculosis · measures hypersensitivity to tuberculin, not immunity · read 48–72 h later · positive = induration
two-step: positive → infected, no 2nd TST, evaluate for disease · negative → retest in 1–3 weeks
BCG vaccine → false positive · induration size ≠ likelihood of active disease, but correlates with future risk
he asked: "Has anybody ever [been] vaccinated for TB?" → the U.S. doesn't vaccinate: a mediocre vaccine that also gives you a positive TB test, so "we have a very good screening program" instead.
FYI only — the 5 / 10 / 15 mm cut-offs (slide 82): "I don't expect you guys to know this, but just be aware that it does matter who you are" (e.g. HIV → a small reaction matters).

12 · upper airway & vascular: rhinitis, decongestants, cough/cold, PE, pulmonary HTN

allergic rhinitis (slide 86) — "this is kind of obvious"
upper-airway inflammation: nasal vessels dilate and become permeable · airborne allergens bind IgE on mast cells → histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins
S&S: sneezing, rhinorrhea, pruritus, congestion, erythema · seasonal (hay fever; outdoor pollen, fungi — spring & fall) vs perennial (dust mites, pet dander)
Tx: intranasal glucocorticoids · antihistamines (oral, intranasal) · sympathomimetic decongestants
intranasal glucocorticoids (slide 87)
suppress congestion, rhinorrhea, itching, erythema · daily dosing · SE: drying of nasal mucosa, burning, nosebleed · e.g. fluticasone, mometasone
sympathomimetic decongestants (slide 88)
activate α₁ receptors on nasal blood vessels → vasoconstriction · "do nothing to the immune response" — just shrink the vessels and dry you up
topical (drops, sprays): act faster, ↑ risk of rebound congestion · oral: act longer, ↑ risk of systemic effects
SE: CNS excitation, restlessness, irritability, insomnia, anxiety · hazardous in HTN / CVD · banned sports substance
pseudoephedrine: behind the counter, ID scanned ("Breaking Bad"); Olympic disqualifications; elite 1500-m runners ~6 s faster

common cold (slide 91)
50–80% rhinovirus · rhinorrhea, congestion, cough, sore throat, hoarseness, headache, malaise, myalgias · self-limiting, no cure · zinc lozenges shorten it (SE nausea; avoid citrus) · treat symptoms: decongestants, antitussives, expectorants, NSAIDs
antihistamines do not work — a cold "is not a type 1 immune response… you're not going to get a histamine release"
pulmonary embolism (slide 92)
a thrombus, embolus, tissue fragment, lipid or air bubble blocks part of the pulmonary vascular bed · source: lower-leg DVT
risks: immobilization, heart failure, surgery, birth-control pills, inherited clotting disorders, malignancy
S&S: pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, tachycardia, anxiety, fever · Tx: anticoagulants, fibrinolytics ("bust the clots")
pulmonary hypertension (slide 93)
mean pulmonary artery pressure > 20 mm Hg · secondary: ↑ pulmonary venous pressure, ↑ flow, vascular obstruction, hypoxemia (COPD) · primary: vessel walls thicken and constrict
S&S (found late): fatigue, chest discomfort, tachypnea, dyspnea, peripheral edema · dx: right-heart catheterization · Tx: diuretics, anticoagulants, CCBs, treat the cause (more with Dr. Schwind)
cor pulmonale = right-ventricular enlargement
constant mismatch / constriction (asthma, COPD) → smooth-muscle hypertrophy → ↑ lung pressure → RV hypertrophy → right-sided failure
