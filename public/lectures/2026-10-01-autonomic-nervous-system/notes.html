1 · rest & digest vs fight or flight

FYI — synaptic transmission (slides 4–6): "more kind of conceptual, so don't feel like you need to know the names." Steps: synthesis → storage → release → receptor binding → termination (reuptake, enzymatic degradation, diffusion). Drugs act on the axon or on synaptic transmission/receptors — every way of meddling ends in one of two outcomes: ↑ or ↓ receptor activation.
ANS — 3 principal functions (slide 7)
  • regulate the heart
  • regulate secretory glands (salivary, gastric, sweat, bronchial)
  • regulate smooth muscle (bronchi, blood vessels, urogenital, GI)
  • maintains homeostasis · subconscious, affected by emotional state · innervates every organ except skeletal muscle
parasympathetic (slide 8) — "sitting next to a fire, reading a book": slows HR · ↑ gastric secretions · empties bladder & bowel · near vision · constricts pupil · contracts bronchial smooth muscle ("I don't need wide-open [airways]")
sympathetic (slide 9) — not only fight-or-flight; a constant "push and pull": blood flow to the brain · redistributing blood · compensating for blood loss (keeps you alive when hemorrhaging) · temperature (skin flow, sweat, piloerection) · fight-or-flight: ↑ HR, BP, shunt blood from skin & viscera, dilate bronchi & pupils, mobilize stored energy
she asked: "Piloerection — which is the fancy name for…?" → goosebumps — "you get to look bigger."
key concept (slide 10)
SNS prepares the body to respond to physiologic demand · PNS supports maintenance, digestion, elimination, restoration

patterns of innervation (slide 11)
  • dual, opposed — most organs (heart: PNS slows, SNS accelerates)
  • dual, complementary — erection & ejaculation: the only time the two work toward the same goal
  • one division only — blood vessels: all sympathetic, no parasympathetic input
autonomic tone (slide 12)

baseline control even at rest. Arterioles, veins, sweat glands → sympathetic; heart, iris, ciliary muscle, GI, bladder, salivary glands → parasympathetic predominates.

✎ blocking a division removes its resting influence. Block α₁ on vessels and "it's not making it relax, it's just removing the signal that told it to constrict" — pulling back on the gas pedal.

two nicotinic subtypes (slide 14)
Nn — N for nerve: autonomic ganglia (both divisions) → activates the postganglionic neuron
Nm — N for muscle: neuromuscular junction of skeletal muscle

"N for nerve, M for muscle — but it's hard to hear that differentiation in speech." Both nicotinic, but different enough that different drugs target them.

student asked: "Are Nn and Nm both ion channels?" → yes — fast muscle response, fast transfer between neurons. Muscarinic receptors are the ones at the parasympathetic target organs.
✎ somatic motor is a one-neuron system: "I'm moving my pinky toe right now… that's a huge long axon coming from my spinal cord."
nicotinic vs muscarinic — the "why" (notes only)
nicotinic = simple ion channels → fast ("we want to move when we want to move")
muscarinic = G-protein-linked → slower, bigger changes at the target organ ("slow down the heart rate")
sweat glands: "the weird odd ball out"
controlled by the sympathetic system but use ACh at muscarinic receptors. Doesn't matter physiologically — but with drugs, "sweating will follow the opposite of what you expect": cholinergic drugs → sweating; anticholinergics → ↓ sweating.
SNS: catabolic — mobilizes resources · generalized response
PNS: anabolic — conservation, renewal, storage · localized response
FYI — "things that don't matter as much" (slides 13, 16): SNS preganglionic cell bodies T1–L2, short fibers to the paravertebral chain; PNS from the brainstem (cranial nerves) + sacral 2–4, long fibers to ganglia near or in the organ. Her point: the body can release transmitter "just right here," but a drug given systemically hits every receptor.

α₂ — decrease sympathetic output (slide 19)
presynaptic on sympathetic neurons + in the CNS · the negative feedback: NE hits α₂ on its own nerve terminal → "we just released some, slow down" → less NE released
→ ↓ sympathetic stimulation of heart & vessels · ↓ vascular tone · ↓ BP · sometimes ↓ HR
✎ α₂ is "the brother that's always like, no, don't."
student asked: (what α₂ activation is used for) → ↓ BP — it tells NE-releasing cells to "slow down a little"; also an adjunct for ADHD, PTSD and other off-label uses.
student asked: "Does blocking just remove the brake — would it have the opposite effect?" → "Exactly" — depends on the drug; vessels have no parasympathetic input, so pulling back the sympathetic → vasodilation.
student asked: "So an α₂ antagonist would do the opposite?" → "Theoretically yes, but we don't have any drugs that do that" (slide 73: ↑ NE release*).
β₁ — think HEART (and kidney) (slide 20)

β₂ — think smooth muscle relaxation (slide 21)
bronchodilation → ↓ airway resistance · vasodilation in selected beds (low yield) · uterine relaxation (can help stop early labor)
metabolic: ↑ glycogenolysis, ↑ blood glucose ("problematic for patients who are diabetic") · K⁺ moves into cells
her line (slide 20)
"We have one heart (β₁) and two lungs (β₂)."
muscarinic — think PNS target organs (slide 22)

baroreceptor reflex (slide 23)
"baro" = pressure, like a barometer. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch sense BP ↑ or ↓ → message to the brain → ANS changes heart rate and vessel tone.
pharm connection: drugs that suddenly change vascular tone can trigger the compensation.
don't forget the reflex
↓ BP → reflex tachycardia
↑ BP → reflex bradycardia
why the heart answers (00:36:05)
BP = CO × SVR · CO = HR × SV

"If my constriction has gone up, I can decrease my cardiac output by lowering my heart rate to balance out that equation."

5 · more vs less cholinergic: bethanechol & atropine (+ Beers)

three ways to change cholinergic activity (slide 27)
  • ↑ direct muscarinic agonists — activate muscarinic receptors (bethanechol)
  • ↑ cholinesterase inhibitors — stop ACh breakdown → ↑ ACh (section 6)
  • ↓ muscarinic antagonists — keep ACh off the receptor (atropine)

cholinergic drugs → MORE of the muscarinic effects · anticholinergics → LESS

↑ muscarinic activity (slide 28)
↓ HR · miosis · bronchoconstriction + ↑ secretions · ↑ GI motility & secretions · urination · ↑ salivation, lacrimation, sweating*
*sweat glands are sympathetic but use ACh at muscarinic receptors — the drug "doesn't care," it finds the receptor.
→ bradycardia + secretions + bronchoconstriction + diarrhea + urination
BETHANECHOL — direct muscarinic agonist (slides 29–30)
aka cholinergic / parasympathomimetic · mimics ACh at PNS target organs · ↑ detrusor contraction → bladder emptying
use: nonobstructive urinary retention — urine is there, the bladder can't contract well
adverse: bradycardia, hypotension · bronchoconstriction, ↑ secretions · cramping, diarrhea, ↑ GI secretions · salivation, sweating · urinary urgency
avoid: mechanical GI or urinary obstruction · significant bradycardia/hypotension · conditions where bronchoconstriction is dangerous (severe asthma, COPD)
monitor: BP & HR · urine output/emptying · abdominal symptoms · respiratory status in at-risk patients
give on an empty stomach to reduce nausea/vomiting · severe cholinergic effects → atropine
✎ "Make sure there's a place for the urine to go." A kidney stone stuck in the urethra + a drug that squeezes the bladder → bladder rupture; a bowel obstruction → bowel rupture.
ATROPINE — muscarinic antagonist (slides 31–32)
aka anticholinergic / parasympatholytic · competitively blocks muscarinic receptors → ↓ parasympathetic effects
uses: symptomatic bradycardia · cholinergic toxicity · ↓ secretions in procedures · eye procedures
key concept: it does not stimulate the heart — it removes the parasympathetic "brake" → ↑ HR
atropine = dry + fast + retention (slide 50)
eyes: mydriasis, blurred vision, photophobia · heart: tachycardia · dry mouth, thick respiratory secretions · constipation, urinary retention · ↓ sweating → heat intolerance / hyperthermia · CNS: confusion/agitation in older adults
contraindications & precautions
  • narrow-angle glaucoma · urinary retention / BPH · GI obstruction or very slow GI motility · tachyarrhythmias
  • older adults are "much more sensitive" — other anticholinergic drugs add up (cumulative burden)
nursing
  • monitor HR, bowel & bladder function, cognition
  • oral care for dry mouth — chronic dryness damages teeth and oral tissue
  • hydration if not contraindicated · avoid overheating (can't sweat during strenuous activity)
  • review prescription and OTC meds for anticholinergic effects
Beers list (slide 33) — "way less fun than it sounds"
drugs that are potentially dangerous for adults > 65 — ↑ rate of adverse drug reactions. Anticholinergics are on it.
FYI: "you don't need to know the ins and outs" of the table.
↑ cholinergic↑ anticholinergic (slide 34 chart)
miosis · ↑ salivation/secretions · bradycardia · bronchoconstriction · ↑ GI motility · ↑ bladder emptying · ↑ sweatingmydriasis · dry mouth/↓ secretions · tachycardia · ↓ bronchial secretions · constipation · urinary retention · ↓ sweating

PARALYSIS ≠ SEDATION ≠ ANALGESIA
blockers give no unconsciousness, amnesia, sedation or pain relief — patients need separate sedation and analgesia. "Horror stories" of patients paralyzed but not sedated during surgery — "that's why anesthesiologists make the big bucks."
✎ the Vanderbilt case: a nurse meant to pull a sedative (Versed) for an older patient in MRI, pulled a paralytic instead, and the patient died. "It will kill the patient if you don't have a way to make them breathe."
student asked: "Do nurses need another nurse to cosign for medications like these?" → cosigning is mostly for wasting narcotics (a 5 mg dose from a 10 mg vial); she didn't think blockers require it — the Vanderbilt case involved multiple failures.
ROCURONIUM · VECURONIUM — nondepolarizing (slide 41)
"end in -curonium" · competitively block Nm → ACh can't bind → no depolarization → flaccid paralysis
adverse: apnea/respiratory paralysis · residual weakness · CV effects vary
prolonged/increased by: general anesthetics · aminoglycosides (gentamicin) · magnesium
nursing: ventilatory support immediately available · sedation + analgesia · monitor respiratory status & depth of blockade · eye, skin, positioning care (corneas dry out, pressure injuries) · verify recovery before stopping respiratory support ("make sure the patient can breathe before we pull that tube out")
reversal: NEOSTIGMINE (slides 42, 48)
AChE inhibitor → ↓ ACh breakdown → ↑ ACh at the NMJ → ACh competes more effectively with the blocker for Nm receptors
reverses nondepolarizing blockade · watch muscarinic effects — an antimuscarinic is commonly coadministered · verify neuromuscular recovery

8 · sux, hyperkalemia, malignant hyperthermia & botox

SUCCINYLCHOLINE ("sux") — depolarizing (slide 43)
Nm agonist → initial depolarization → brief fasciculations → persistent depolarization → flaccid paralysis

use: rapid, short-duration paralysis when rapid airway control/intubation is needed — faster, so used more in the ER.

  • respiratory paralysis — immediate ventilation must be possible
  • hyperkalemia — depolarization moves K⁺ out of muscle → ↑ serum K⁺
  • malignant hyperthermia in genetically susceptible people
  • bradycardia · fasciculations · postoperative muscle pain
Not tested: slide 43's last bullet (prolonged paralysis with impaired pseudocholinesterase) — "just cross off that last one."
hyperkalemia risk groups (slide 44)
upregulated extrajunctional ACh receptors: major burns after the acute phase · major crush injury/trauma · spinal cord injury · denervation · prolonged immobilization · certain neuromuscular disorders
↑ Nm receptors → more channels open → more K⁺ out → severe hyperkalemia → life-threatening dysrhythmias. "Their bodies are a little more primed."
MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA (slide 44)
rare, life-threatening pharmacogenetic hypermetabolic reaction · triggers: succinylcholine + certain volatile inhaled anesthetics
abnormal Ca²⁺ release in skeletal muscle → sustained muscle activity + huge ↑ metabolism → rapid ↑ CO₂, rigidity, tachycardia, acidosis, hyperkalemia, rising temp ("105, 106 degrees")
early vs late
early: rapidly rising end-tidal CO₂ + muscle rigidity (CO₂ reflects metabolism)
late: hyperthermia

treat: stop triggering agents · supportive care ("buckets of ice," cooling blankets) · DANTROLENE = the antidote

✎ the pre-op question "has anyone in your family had a bad reaction to anesthesia?" is the MH screen.
sux safety (slide 45)
  • avoid/extreme caution: personal or family history of MH · existing hyperkalemia · conditions with dangerous K⁺ release or prolonged paralysis
  • sux + volatile inhaled anesthetics → ↑ MH risk in susceptible people
  • ventilation · sedation/analgesia · assess K⁺ & MH risk · monitor CV/respiratory · recognize early MH · dantrolene rapidly available wherever triggers are used
BOTULINUM TOXIN (slide 46)
prevents presynaptic ACh release → ACh can't reach receptors → ↓ muscle contraction / cholinergic activity
uses: focal spasticity/dystonia · chronic migraine (injected at the base of the skull) · blepharospasm · hyperhidrosis (sweat glands are muscarinic) · selected bladder disorders · cosmetic
adverse: usually local weakness; rare systemic spread → generalized weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise · other drugs that impair NM transmission ↑ weakness

EPINEPHRINE — nonselective α/β agonist (slides 53–54)
uses: anaphylaxis · cardiac arrest · severe bronchospasm (selected) · added to local anesthetics for local vasoconstriction
adverse (too much adrenergic): tachycardia, palpitations, dysrhythmias, HTN, angina, tremor, anxiety, hyperglycemia
in anaphylaxis there is no absolute contraindication; caution with CV disease in other settings · other sympathomimetics ↑ HTN, tachycardia, dysrhythmias
nursing: HR, BP, rhythm, response · anaphylaxis → IM, anterolateral thigh · monitor airway · teach autoinjector use + emergency evaluation after any use
anaphylaxis does…
mediator release → vasodilation + ↑ vascular permeability → hypotension + tissue edema
and bronchoconstriction + airway edema → impaired ventilation
epinephrine answers…
α₁ → vasoconstriction → ↑ BP + ↓ mucosal edema
β₁ → ↑ HR + contractility
β₂ → bronchodilation → improved airflow
NOREPINEPHRINE (Levophed) — vasopressor (slide 55)
strong α₁, some β₁ → peripheral vasoconstriction → ↑ SVR → ↑ BP
use: severe hypotension/shock needing a vasopressor — especially distributive/septic shock (vasodilation is the problem)
adverse: HTN · dysrhythmias · ↓ peripheral perfusion · ischemia · extravasation → tissue injury/necrosis
precaution: vasoconstriction doesn't replace volume — recognize and address hypovolemia
nursing: continuous BP/hemodynamics · rhythm · peripheral perfusion · watch the IV site · titrate to the prescribed target
PHENYLEPHRINE — selective α₁ agonist (slide 56)
α₁ → vascular smooth muscle contraction → vasoconstriction
uses (by route): ↑ BP in hypotension · nasal decongestion · mydriasis
adverse: HTN · headache · reduced perfusion · reflex bradycardia (BP jumps → baroreceptors slow the heart)
caution: significant HTN, conditions worsened by vasoconstriction
nursing: BP & HR · perfusion when systemic · teach: avoid excessive/prolonged OTC nasal spray use

10 · adrenergic agonists II: dobutamine, albuterol, clonidine

Not covered in the recording — from slides 57–62 and the handouts.
DOBUTAMINE — β₁ (slide 57)
β₁ → ↑ contractility → ↑ stroke volume → ↑ cardiac output (may ↑ HR too)
use: short-term support — acute HF, cardiogenic shock, low-output states
adverse: tachycardia, dysrhythmias, angina, BP changes, ↑ myocardial O₂ demand
caution: dysrhythmias, ischemic heart disease · β-blockers ↓ its effect
nursing: continuous HR/rhythm, BP, perfusion, chest pain
ALBUTEROL (Ventolin) — SABA, β₂ (slide 58)
β₂ → bronchial smooth muscle relaxes → bronchodilation
use: rapid relief of bronchospasm — the rescue inhaler
adverse: tremor, nervousness, tachycardia/palpitations, hypokalemia
β-blockers ↓ bronchodilation · other sympathomimetics ↑ CV effects
teach: inhaler technique · rescue vs maintenance · frequent rescue use = poor disease control
CLONIDINE (Catapres) — central α₂ (slide 59)
central presynaptic α₂ → ↓ NE release → ↓ sympathetic outflow → ↓ HR + ↓ vascular tone → ↓ BP
use: hypertension
adverse: hypotension, bradycardia, sedation, dizziness, dry mouth
antihypertensives → additive hypotension · CNS depressants → additive sedation
teach: change positions slowly · warn about sedation · adherence · taper
key concept (slide 57)
↑ contractility = ↑ myocardial O₂ demand
β₂ stimulation → Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase on → K⁺ shifts into cells → ↓ serum K⁺

albuterol hypokalemia is most likely with higher systemic exposure (slide 58).

clonidine: DO NOT STOP ABRUPTLY
sudden stop → rebound sympathetic activity → severe hypertension → tachycardia, headache, nervousness

PRAZOSIN (Minipress) — α₁ antagonist (slide 65)
blocks α₁ on vascular smooth muscle → vasodilation → ↓ SVR → ↓ BP · use: hypertension
adverse: hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, weakness, syncope, reflex tachycardia
other antihypertensives/vasodilators → additive hypotension
FIRST-DOSE EFFECT
significant orthostatic hypotension/syncope after the first dose or a big dose increase → give the first dose at bedtime, rise slowly, fall precautions
TAMSULOSIN (Flomax) — selective α₁A (slide 66)
α₁A in prostate & bladder-neck smooth muscle → relaxation → ↓ resistance → improved urine flow · use: BPH
adverse: dizziness, orthostatic hypotension, headache, abnormal ejaculation · additive hypotension with BP-lowering drugs
teach: change positions slowly · it improves flow but does not shrink the prostate

12 · β-blockers, masking hypoglycemia + receptor review

Not covered in the recording — from slides 67–75 and the handouts.
PROPRANOLOL (Inderal) — nonselective (slide 68)
β₁ block: ↓ HR, contractility, AV conduction, renin · β₂ block: bronchoconstriction risk
uses: HTN, angina, dysrhythmias, migraine prevention, essential tremor, selected hyperthyroid manifestations
avoid/caution: significant bradycardia, heart block, decompensated HF, asthma/bronchospastic disease
opposes β₂ agonists like albuterol
METOPROLOL (Lopressor/Toprol-XL) — cardioselective (slide 69)
preferentially β₁: heart ↓ HR, contractility, conduction; kidney ↓ renin
uses: HTN, angina, selected dysrhythmias, post-MI, HF (appropriate formulations)
caution: bradycardia, heart block, acute/decompensated HF
selectivity is not absolute (study guide)
CARVEDILOL (Coreg) — α₁ + nonselective β (slide 70)
β₁ block → ↓ HR, ↓ cardiac workload · α₁ block → vasodilation, ↓ SVR
uses: heart failure, HTN, selected post-MI with ventricular dysfunction
adverse: bradycardia, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, fatigue, bronchospasm risk
watch for worsening HF when started/titrated
