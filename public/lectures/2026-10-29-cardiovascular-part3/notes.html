1 · supply vs demand · collateral circulation

Myocardial ischemia = oxygen supply is insufficient to meet oxygen demand (slide 4).

O₂ demand ↑ with…
heart rate — faster HR, more O₂ consumed
contractility — "the stronger it has to be, … the more energy"
wall stress — ↑ preload (more venous return) or ↑ afterload (higher systemic BP)
O₂ supply depends on…
coronary blood flow — patent arteries, perfusion pressure (plaques or spasm ↓ it)
diastolic filling time
O₂ content — hemoglobin & saturation
perfusion happens in diastole
Most LV coronary perfusion occurs during diastole — in systole "there's so much squeezing going on" that flow is poor. A very fast HR shortens diastole → ↓ supply and ↑ demand.
Kahoot: which change increases myocardial oxygen demand? → (froze before the answer; slide 4) ↑ heart rate, ↑ contractility or ↑ wall stress
Kahoot: coronary perfusion occurs mostly during… → diastole — a really quick heartbeat shortens diastolic filling time and can compromise coronary flow.
Kahoot: what best describes myocardial ischemia? → O₂ supply is insufficient for demand
✎ Patho → Pharm: antianginal drugs work by reducing O₂ demand, improving O₂ supply, or both.
healthy heart vs CAD (slide 4)
1At rest — supply and demand balanced in both.
2Exertion, healthy heart — demand rises (exercise, stress) and supply rises to match: no angina.
3Exertion, severe CAD — the plaque can't let supply rise → balance tips → angina pain.
collateral circulation (slide 5)
Collateral vessels = alternate pathways for blood to reach myocardium.
A slowly growing plaque ("the growth, not the rupture") can stimulate new vessels from another branch.
Varies a lot between people and takes time — "wouldn't it be nice if we could figure out exactly why some patients do that."
Limits ischemic injury — the same blockage leaves a smaller infarct — but usually can't compensate for a sudden, complete occlusion.
Kahoot: why may collateral circulation protect some myocardium? → another pathway for blood to get past the blockage.

2 · angina: stable, variant, unstable

Angina pectoris ("most people just drop the pectoris") = chest discomfort caused by transient myocardial ischemia (slide 6).

  • pressure, squeezing, heaviness or discomfort in the chest
  • may radiate to the arm ("especially the left arm"), shoulder, jaw, neck or back — under the sternum, the back, the throat (slide 8)
  • may come with dyspnea, nausea, diaphoresis
ischemia — inadequate O₂ delivery; potentially reversible. Cells are struggling, painful, but not dying.
infarction — prolonged ischemia → irreversible myocardial cell death.
key concept (slide 6)
Angina indicates myocardial ischemia — but ischemia does not necessarily mean MI.
student asked: how does overall anemia affect angina? → it ↓ O₂ content body-wide; with a blockage it definitely makes it worse, but there's no direct cause-and-effect — long before it kills myocytes they'd be too short of breath to get out of bed.
type (slide 7)mechanismpattern
stablefixed coronary narrowing; ↑ demand exceeds supplypredictable with exertion; relieved by rest or nitroglycerin
variant (Prinzmetal, vasospastic)transient coronary artery vasospasmoften at rest; responds to vasodilators

a plaque turning unstable, clotting starting · "knocking on the door" of the big heart attack
"a pre-warning that things are about to go pretty bad" — a medical emergency
Kahoot: stable angina most often occurs because of… → a fixed atherosclerotic narrowing — "that one was pretty easy."
Kahoot: variant angina is most directly caused by… → (slide 10) transient coronary vasospasm — the muscle around the vessel, not plaque.
✎ her pearl: if rest + nitro don't relieve it, "now we're looking at this as possibly unstable angina."
FYI only — the ECG changes in variant angina: "You don't need to know exactly what is happening."

3 · NBC part 1 — nitrates

NBC "if you remember the network"
  • Nnitrates — primarily venodilation → ↓ preload
  • Bbeta blockers — ↓ HR & contractility
  • Ccalcium channel blockers — arteriolar dilation and/or ↓ cardiac activity; some relieve vasospasm

All three ↓ myocardial O₂ demand (slides 11, 15). These are for stable angina — "the vast majority of the people with angina."

prototypes (slide 12)
nitroglycerin
Nitrostat, Nitro-Dur · sublingual = rapid relief of acute angina — "the one that someone takes when they have angina"
isosorbide mononitrate
Imdur ("way easier to say") · long-acting = prevention of recurrent angina through the day
the nitrate pathway (slide 12)
NO → venodilation → ↓ venous return → ↓ preload → ↓ wall stress → ↓ O₂ demand

Also dilates coronary arteries a bit and can relieve vasospasm — so it's sometimes given for variant angina too. Also used in selected ACS.

Kahoot: nitroglycerin primarily lowers O₂ demand by… → venodilation → ↓ preload ("primarily a venodilator"); a little coronary vasodilation too.
SL nitro teaching — "clear teaching"
1Sit down, rest, take one dose.
2Not relieved after the first dose → call EMS now.
3Additional doses may be taken as directed while awaiting help.
Why: the plaque could be turning unstable and rupture — "now we have an actual cardiac event occurring."
student asked: how long does it take to help? → "Five minutes, I believe" — and they should be resting, not carrying on with the activity.
adverse effectwhy
headachevasodilation
hypotension, dizziness↓ preload ↓ BP overall
reflex tachycardiaresponse to the BP drop
never with PDE-5 inhibitors ✎
sildenafil (Viagra), tadalafil — the erectile-dysfunction drugs — also vasodilate → profound hypotension. "This comes up a lot in boards."
Kahoot: which nitrate combination can cause profound hypotension? → nitrate + sildenafil — "right, good."
student asked: what's the washout time between them? → she wasn't sure — but the ED asks very specifically before giving any nitrate.
  • monitor BP and symptoms of hypotension; caution if already hypotensive
  • caution / avoid in suspected right ventricular infarction — ↓ preload starves an RV that's "already struggling to push blood forward"
  • tolerance with continuous use → long-acting regimens need a daily nitrate-free interval — usually at night, "hopefully at night they're resting and sleeping"
Kahoot: why is a nitrate-free interval sometimes prescribed? → (slide 12) to prevent nitrate tolerance — "if someone took it 24 hours a day, it would eventually stop working."

4 · NBC part 2 — beta blockers & CCBs · long-term plan

beta blockers — metoprolol (Lopressor, Toprol-XL) · slide 13
Block cardiac β₁ → ↓ HR and ↓ contractility → ↓ O₂ demand.
Slower HR → longer diastole → can ↑ coronary perfusion.
uses: prevent chronic stable angina · selected post-MI clients (esp. reduced EF) · HTN, some dysrhythmias
adverse: bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue, AV conduction abnormalities
nursing: monitor HR & BP · avoid abrupt discontinuation — rebound sympathetic activity can precipitate ischemia · avoid in isolated vasospastic angina — "the only new thing here"
Kahoot: metoprolol reduces angina mainly by… → ↓ HR and ↓ contractility — she expected you to know the beta blockers by now.
long-term management of stable angina (slide 16)
relieve ischemic symptoms
nitrates · beta blockers · CCBs
reduce CV risk
statins (lipid-lowering) · antiplatelets in selected clients · ACEI/ARB with certain comorbidities · smoking, HTN, diabetes, activity
✎ Patho → Pharm: antianginals reduce symptoms; preventive therapies address the underlying atherosclerosis and thrombosis — "try and prevent things from getting worse."
Kahoot: which treatment mainly lowers future atherosclerotic risk rather than immediately relieving angina? → high-intensity statin — "your long-term one"; SL nitro and coronary vasodilators are the immediate ones.

CCBs block L-type Ca²⁺ channels → less Ca²⁺ into vascular smooth muscle and/or heart cells. Vessels need Ca²⁺ to constrict → block it → vasodilation (slide 14).

dihydropyridines — amlodipine (Norvasc)
"the class ends in -pine" · periphery: arteriolar dilation → ↓ afterload → ↓ O₂ demand · relieves vasospasm · minimal effect on HR & AV conduction
adverse: peripheral edema, headache, flushing — edema "usually worrisome, but in this case, it's expected"
non-DHP — verapamil (Calan), diltiazem (Cardizem)
"no good way to remember them… you just have to put these ones together" · heart: ↓ HR, ↓ AV nodal conduction, ↓ contractility — like a beta blocker · also some vasodilation
adverse: bradycardia, AV block, hypotension · verapamil: constipation — it likes the gut's Ca²⁺ channels too (↓ peristalsis)

uses: chronic stable angina · vasospastic angina · selected HTN or supraventricular dysrhythmias

Kahoot: which drug primarily reduces afterload with little direct AV-node effect? → amlodipine — "the -pines" prefer the periphery; verapamil and diltiazem "like the heart better."
Kahoot: which adverse effect is especially associated with verapamil/diltiazem? → bradycardia / AV block — they slow AV-node conduction and HR.
Kahoot: which class is especially useful for preventing coronary vasospasm? → (slide 10) calcium channel blockers — they make coronary vessels "less squeezy."
high-yield safety (slide 17)
nitrates + PDE-5 → severe hypotension · β-blockers & non-DHP CCBs → bradycardia

5 · ACS: plaque rupture → clot · UA vs NSTEMI vs STEMI

the ACS cascade (slide 19)
plaque rupture or erosion → subendothelial collagen & tissue factor exposed → platelets adhere, activate, aggregate + coagulation cascade → thrombus partly or fully blocks flow → myocardial ischemia
✎ her pearl: the body sees the ruptured plaque and goes "okay, I've got to cover this up — it's like ripping off a scab." A little clot → unstable angina; a big or long-lasting one → heart attack.
reversible ischemia
brief or less severe (partial, transient occlusion) · cells stay viable · no necrosis
e.g., unstable angina, early NSTEMI
prolonged / severe ischemia
persistent occlusion · cell death (necrosis) · MI
e.g., STEMI, NSTEMI with infarction
Kahoot: ACS commonly begins with which event? → (slide 19) plaque rupture or erosion
the plaque spectrum (slide 22)

"Most of us have plaques… but most of us are asymptomatic." Asymptomatic plaque → stable fixed plaque (stable angina) → unstable plaque + platelet aggregation (unstable angina) → thrombus (NSTEMI / STEMI).

partial = NSTEMI, complete = STEMI? too simple
"You could have a total occlusion of a smaller vessel and still have an NSTEMI." It comes down to how much tissue is affected.
student asked: is the "widow maker" a STEMI? → that's about location — the left anterior descending; probably a STEMI, but a small LAD branch could be an NSTEMI. How much heart the artery supplies decides the damage.

unstable angina is still an emergency (slide 23)
Usually acute plaque disruption + thrombus: flow ↓ enough for ischemia, no detectable necrosis. New-onset angina, worsening stable angina, or angina at rest ("just wakes up with chest pain"). Unpredictable, prolonged; "very likely" to progress to MI "in the near future if we don't do something" → urgent evaluation, ECG, serial troponins.
student asked: how does troponin leave the blood? → the body clears it naturally (filtered by the kidneys) — she wasn't sure of the chemistry.

6 · chest pain workup · treating UA/NSTEMI

chest pain → working diagnosis ACS (slides 21, 26)
1ECG first — "it's really quick to throw on some leads."
2ST elevation → STEMI → cath lab (or lytics). "We don't care about the rest of it."
3No ST elevation (normal or other changes) = NSTE-ACS → look at troponin.
4Troponin rise/fall → NSTEMI. Troponin stays normal on serial checks → unstable angina.
serial, serial ✎
"For someone with ACS, we're doing serial ECGs… and we also do serial troponins." An early or evolving MI can look normal at first — "just because it's not elevated right now doesn't mean things aren't getting worse."
student asked: how fast do troponins come back? → very fast when the lab knows it's urgent — she wouldn't give an exact number.
student asked: does troponin rise and then fall? → yes — so we trend it down; if it starts rising again, something new is happening.
✎ her story: on The Pitt (season 2) the leads were placed badly under a woman's breast and the STEMI didn't show — re-placed, and there it was. "You've got to stop being afraid of breasts."
FYI only — identifying ST elevation or the other waveforms on a strip (slide 25): "you don't have to identify it, at least not this semester… It's conceptual." The Calgary pathogenesis diagram (slide 28) is for enjoyment.

goals: relieve ischemia · prevent more thrombus · reduce further injury · find who needs angiography/revascularization

7 · MI: ischemia → infarction · presentation · troponin · consequences

ischemia (slide 27)
↓ O₂ → ↓ aerobic ATP · contractile function deteriorates · electrical instability · restore perfusion early → cells may recover
infarction
severe or prolonged ischemia → irreversible injury & necrosis · contents incl. troponin released · lost myocardium → ↓ contractility, may ↓ CO
presentation (slide 31)
  • chest pressure, tightness, heaviness — often persistent or at rest; radiates to arm, shoulder, jaw, neck, back
  • dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, unusual fatigue
  • minimal or no chest discomfort — especially older adults and clients with diabetes
  • women more likely to report nausea, dyspnea, fatigue — though chest discomfort is still common (slide 32: cold sweat, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath)
✎ her story: her mom's MI ~18 years ago "thought she had the flu" — no chest pain, just vomiting, sweating, weak. A month later the scar became inflamed and she came in with chest pain. "Any chest pain we're going to be concerned about until we know otherwise."
Kahoot: which statement about MI symptoms is most accurate? → some clients (older, diabetic) have minimal or no chest pain — she laughed at "MI never occurs at rest."
FYI only — the Calgary MI diagram (slide 33): "for you to enjoy if you'd like to."
troponin (slide 34)
Contractile protein inside cardiac cells → released when they're injured.
Elevated = myocardial injury, not the cause. Other causes: myocarditis (inflammation kills cells), small amounts in heart failure ("cells slowly dying off one by one"). In MI the rise is usually far more intense, with sudden symptoms.
Serial troponins catch an acute rise or fall = new injury; keep trending.
Acute MI = troponin + symptoms + ECG + other evidence of ischemia.
key concept
Troponin identifies injury; the ECG distinguishes STEMI from NSTEMI — not how high the troponin goes (slide 41).
Kahoot: does an elevated troponin by itself establish the cause? → no — something is injuring myocardium; context says what.
Kahoot: why are serial troponin measurements useful? → (slide 34) to catch an acute rise or fall of new injury
student asked: would troponin still be up a month later? → it sticks around a while (she thought ~14 days, not sure); for her mom, the ECG changes showed the old MI.
consequences of MI (slide 35)
loss of contractile myocardium → ↓ SV & CO · ↓ EF (esp. LV) → heart failure / cardiogenic shock
  • electrical instability → dysrhythmias — "to make the situation even more fun"
  • ventricular remodeling → progressive dysfunction — next lecture is heart failure
  • bigger infarct → more symptoms; depends on location and extent
Kahoot: loss of functioning myocardium after MI can cause… → (slide 35) ↓ SV & CO, dysrhythmias, HF or cardiogenic shock
FYI only — cardiogenic shock in detail: next semester with the other shocks.

8 · STEMI: restore flow — PCI, fibrinolytics & the rest

she asked: "When we actually have a STEMI, what are we going to do? How are we going to get some blood back?" → restore coronary blood flow ASAP — the cath lab for PCI.
1restore coronary flow — emergency PCI preferred when available in time; fibrinolytics if timely PCI is unavailable and the client is eligible
2prevent more thrombus — antiplatelets (↓ aggregation) + anticoagulants (limit new clot)
3↓ O₂ demand — nitroglycerin if BP allows · beta blockers only in selected, hemodynamically stable clients
4reduce complications — high-intensity statin · meds for ventricular function & comorbidities · O₂ only for hypoxemia
beta blocker caution
Large MI + poor contraction + a drug that ↓ contractility → "we could make the situation worse." In a milder MI it nicely ↓ demand and limits injury.
MONA "the old mnemonic that we taught forever" — outdated
  • Mmorphine
  • Ooxygen — only if hypoxic: "if someone's sitting 96%, you don't need to throw oxygen on them"
  • Nnitrates
  • Aaspirin
problem (slide 40)treatment
coronary obstructionPCI, or fibrinolysis in selected cases
platelet activation & aggregationantiplatelets
ongoing thrombus formationanticoagulants
↑ myocardial O₂ demandnitrates, selected β-blockers
underlying atherosclerosishigh-intensity statin, risk factors

Key concept: each treatment targets a different part of the ACS process — no single drug does it all.

primary PCI (slide 37)
preferred for STEMI when prompt · catheter opens the artery (balloon + stent) · goal: restore flow ASAP to limit necrosis
fibrinolytic therapy
breaks down the fibrin in a thrombus · eligible STEMI when timely PCI isn't available · best early · bleeding risk limits who can get it
time is myocardium ✎
Reperfusion saves ischemic myocardium before irreversible death. "The faster you can get blood flow restored, the more tissue you are going to save."
Kahoot: if ischemic myocardium is reperfused before irreversible injury, it may… → recover — "Good. That is our goal."
Kahoot: when promptly available, the preferred STEMI reperfusion method is… → primary PCI
Kahoot: when may fibrinolytic therapy be considered? → (slide 37) eligible STEMI when timely PCI isn't available
Kahoot: why is rapid reperfusion important in STEMI? → to save myocardium before it dies

Slide 38: thrombolysis = enzymatic digestion of the clot · PTCA = physically pushes the plaque aside, then a stent prevents reocclusion · CABG = a new conduit around the blockage ("quadruple bypass" = four new routes). Slide 39: a 99% proximal LAD stenosis before and after a stent. A heart attack "doesn't necessarily mean… the end of the world" — her mom quit smoking and is still going 18 years later.

student asked: how do they know where to go in PCI? → dye shows where blood should flow and isn't; a balloon opens it and a stent keeps it open.
student asked: femoral or radial? → femoral is traditional (lie flat ≥ 6 h, no bending at the waist, more bleeding/clot risk); radial has fewer risks but is slower, so STEMIs more often go femoral.
student asked: recovery from CABG? → a sternotomy is a big, intense recovery — chest tubes, lines; prepare clients for it.
student asked: what if the blockage is huge? → the balloon may not go in; stents come in lengths; local medication stops clotting at the stent, and afterwards the client needs antiplatelets.
FYI only — timing targets (PCI ≤ 90 min, transfer ≤ 120 min, lytics ≤ 30 min of arrival, slide 37): "You do not need to know anything about timing principles." Femoral vs radial and CABG details are adult health.
