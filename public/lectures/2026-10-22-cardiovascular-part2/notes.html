1 · hypertension: the silent killer

HTN = chronic elevation of systemic arterial blood pressure · extremely common · a major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (slide 4).

✎ her pearl: "the silent killer" — "you can be walking around with really high blood pressure… no symptoms… but it's slowly causing a lot of damage internally."
key concept (slide 4)
HTN is dangerous not simply because the pressure is high, but because sustained pressure damages blood vessels and organs over time.

She takes losartan every day herself — "If I don't, I get a headache."

FYI only — "please don't memorize those statistics": leading cause of global mortality, most common primary diagnosis in the US, 45% of US adults hypertensive, only ~24% controlled.

symptoms (slide 5): chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness, weakness, change in vision, difficulty speaking

severe HTN vs emergency — same numbers
The difference is symptoms: the emergency is "these numbers, plus symptoms, that that high blood pressure is acutely causing problems."
Her story: 15 years ago a cyclist with a very high reading at a student BP screening was sent to the ER — "We would not do that anymore." Without symptoms it isn't worth an emergency room.

Why the thresholds dropped: 30 years ago "until we got to 140 over 90, we just kind of didn't care." BP usually creeps up slowly, so now we start acting (mostly diet and lifestyle) once it's over 120/80 — and more aggressively with diabetes and/or renal disease.

primary (essential) — 90–95%
no single identifiable cause · genetics + environment + mechanisms: ↑ SNS activity · ↑ RAAS activity · impaired renal Na⁺ excretion · ↑ SVR
secondary
an identifiable condition or medication: kidney disease / renal artery stenosis · obstructive sleep apnea · endocrine (primary aldosteronism, thyroid) · NSAIDs, corticosteroids, some contraceptives · treat the cause → HTN may resolve
Kahoot: which finding suggests secondary hypertension? → renal artery stenosis — one identifiable cause; fix it and it goes away. High sodium intake is a modifiable contributor to primary HTN ("pretty impossible to have hypertension only because you have a lot of high salt intake"); family history can't be taken away.
student asked: "aren't they all potentially modifiable?" → "They all are potentially modifiable" — the nuance is that inactivity (or salt) alone rarely causes HTN; it's inactivity + genetics + other things = primary.
non-modifiable (slide 9)
family history / genetics · advancing age (vessels lose elasticity — a 90-year-old at 140/90 is "good job," a 20-year-old isn't) · gender (men > women younger; women > men after menopause — estrogen is cardioprotective) · race
modifiable
excess dietary sodium · obesity · inactivity · excess alcohol · unhealthy diet · chronic stress & poor sleep

"Is this really non-modifiable?" The much higher HTN rate in Black Americans is driven by social determinants, not genetics — "we can modify how we attack some of these disparities."

FYI only — slides 7–8 pathogenesis diagrams: "not to memorize, but just to… emphasize that there are a lot of different factors."

3 · diuretics: thiazide, loop, potassium-sparing

All of them: ↑ renal Na⁺ excretion, water follows → ↓ extracellular & circulating volume. BP falls mainly from ↓ volume; some classes also ↓ SVR over time. Shared risks: hypovolemia/hypotension · electrolyte abnormalities · acid–base disturbances (slide 19).

You can be hypotensive and edematous at once (renal, liver or heart failure): too much fluid in the body but not enough in the vessels.

thiazide · hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ, Microzide)
"has thiazide right in it" · ↑ Na⁺ & water excretion · ↓ SVR with long-term use
use: first-line for uncomplicated HTN ("a nice first choice") · mild edema
AE: ↓K⁺ · ↓Na⁺ · dehydration/hypotension · hyperuricemia → gout · may ↑ glucose
nursing: BP, Na⁺, K⁺, renal function · orthostasis · give in the morning
history of gout → "other choices might be a better choice"
loop · furosemide (Lasix) — "our big gun"
most potent Na⁺ & water excretion · ↓ volume and preload
use: fluid overload in heart failure, pulmonary edema, kidney/liver edema, some HTN with volume overload
AE: hypokalemia (stronger) · ↓Na⁺ · hypovolemia · metabolic alkalosis · ototoxicity (high doses or rapid IV)
nursing: BP, fluid status, weight ("the best way to overall track someone's fluid status"), electrolytes, renal function · earlier in the day
FYI for now: hypokalemia ↑ digoxin toxicity — "put that in your back pocket", digoxin comes in two lectures
K⁺-sparing · spironolactone (Aldactone)
blocks aldosterone receptors → ↓ Na⁺ & water reabsorption, ↓ K⁺ excretion · limits aldosterone-driven cardiac remodeling/fibrosis
use: resistant HTN · HFrEF · hyperaldosteronism · added to high-dose Lasix to balance K⁺ loss
AE: hyperkalemia · hypotension · gynecomastia, menstrual irregularities
nursing: K⁺ & renal function · ↑ risk with ACEI, ARB, K⁺ supplements · avoid K⁺ salt substitutes
Lasix = "lasts six"
lasts 6 hours → hospital doses at 9 a.m. and 3 p.m. → out of the system by ~9 p.m. so clients aren't up all night (elderly + night-time bathroom trips = falls)
she asked: "Your patient's potassium level this morning is 3.2, and they have Lasix ordered with their 9 a.m. meds. What should you do?" → don't give it — normal K⁺ is 3.5–5 and Lasix makes them lose more; at least they'd need K⁺ supplements. "Low potassium and Lasix really should be making your brain have some little red flags."
Kahoot: which electrolyte loss is expected with furosemide (Lasix)? → potassium (hypokalemia).
✎ her pearl: salt substitutes — most sodium comes from processed food, and many substitutes swap the salt for potassium: "you're actually getting a whole bunch of extra potassium" on spironolactone without knowing it.
Kahoot: which combination increases hyperkalemia risk? → spironolactone + losartan — K⁺-sparing diuretic + ARB; both ↓ aldosterone effects → K⁺ rises. "Very nice."
student asked: would someone be on spironolactone and an ACE inhibitor? → "They might be" — in heart failure both matter (they block aldosterone and angiotensin II, which drive cardiac remodeling): "we just have to do extra monitoring."
FYI only — "you don't need to memorize what parts of the nephron they're working in" (thiazide: Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransporter, distal tubule · loop: Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻, thick ascending loop of Henle · spironolactone: distal nephron), nor the Lasix IV push rate she quoted from experience. Spironolactone for PCOS — her aside.

4 · sympatholytics: beta, alpha-1, alpha/beta, central alpha-2

Review from the ANS unit. None of the sympatholytics may be stopped abruptly: with less stimulation the body makes more receptors, so stopping suddenly → too many receptors → BP shoots up.

beta blockers · propranolol (Inderal) β₁+β₂ · metoprolol (Lopressor) β₁ "cardioselective"
β₁ heart → ↓ HR & contractility → ↓ CO · β₁ kidney → ↓ renin release → ↓ RAAS
use: HTN with another indication (CAD, selected HF) · angina, dysrhythmias, post-MI · not first-line for uncomplicated HTN
AE: bradycardia, hypotension, AV block · fatigue, dizziness, exercise intolerance · bronchospasm (nonselective) · masks adrenergic signs of hypoglycemia
nursing: HR & BP first — often held if HR < 60 · don't stop abruptly
✎ her pearl: young, healthy, exercising? Not the drug — "this medication keeps your heart rate from being able to get up high… can't improve my time."
Kahoot: metoprolol lowers BP partly by reducing… → (slide 23) heart rate, contractility and renin release.
Kahoot: which concern is especially important with propranolol? → it blocks β₁ and β₂ (non-cardioselective) → bronchospasm.
alpha-1 blocker · prazosin (Minipress)
blocks α₁ on vascular smooth muscle → arteriolar & venous dilation → ↓ SVR
use: HTN (not first-line) · other α₁ blockers for BPH
AE: first-dose orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, syncope, reflex tachycardia · nursing: first-dose precautions, may give at bedtime; rise slowly
Kahoot: a patient taking prazosin should be taught about… → first-dose orthostatic hypotension from the massive vasodilation (tamsulosin blocks the α₁s in the prostate).
combined α/β · carvedilol (Coreg)
α₁ → ↓ SVR · β₁ → ↓ HR, contractility, renin · β₂ → possible bronchoconstriction ("we do not have a formula to only do alpha-1 and beta-1")
use: HFrEF, HTN, LV dysfunction after MI · AE: bradycardia, hypotension, orthostasis, bronchospasm · watch for worsening HF when starting/increasing
Kahoot: carvedilol lowers BP through which two effects? → (slide 25) ↓ CO (β₁) and ↓ SVR (α₁).
central α₂ agonist · clonidine (Catapres)
an adrenergic agonist that ultimately ↓ sympathetic outflow — "alpha-2 is like the one that keeps everyone else in track"
use: HTN when others are insufficient · AE: sedation, dry mouth, bradycardia, hypotension, rebound HTN if stopped abruptly → taper
Kahoot: abruptly stopping clonidine can cause… → rebound hypertension — "with sympatholytics, we don't want to abruptly stop."

5 · vasodilators & calcium channel blockers

the vessel decides (slide 28)
arterioles dilate → ↓ SVR → ↓ afterload
veins dilate → ↓ venous return → ↓ preload
hydralazine (Apresoline)
mainly dilates arterioles → ↓ SVR & afterload · selected HTN incl. some hypertensive emergencies and HTN in pregnancy · often combined with other drugs
AE: reflex tachycardia (baroreceptor reflex → ↑ HR, contractility, O₂ demand) · Na⁺ & water retention (RAAS) · hypotension · drug-induced lupus with prolonged use
nursing: BP, HR, edema · a beta blocker and diuretic may be added
✎ her pearl: on her stroke unit there's often an order — if systolic BP goes above 180, give hydralazine; otherwise regular antihypertensives.
Kahoot: hydralazine can trigger reflex tachycardia because… → BP falls and the baroreceptor reflex kicks in.
FYI only — minoxidil: "just be familiar with the name" (severe resistant HTN; became a hair-growth product) · nitrates (venous → ↓ preload): "hold on to" them for angina next week.

CCBs block L-type Ca²⁺ channels. Vessels: Ca²⁺ in → contraction → block it → arteriolar relaxation → ↓ SVR. Heart muscle: ↓ contractility (negative inotrope). SA/AV nodes: ↓ HR, slower AV conduction.

6 · RAAS drugs & choosing a drug

↓ renal perfusion · ↓ NaCl at macula densa · β₁ stimulation → renin → angiotensinogen → angiotensin I (inactive) → ACE (lungs/endothelium) → angiotensin II
angiotensin II — powerful arteriolar vasoconstriction (↑ SVR) · releases aldosterone · cardiac & vascular remodeling
aldosterone — ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption, water follows (↑ volume) · ↑ K⁺ and H⁺ excretion
ACE inhibitors · lisinopril (Zestril), enalapril — "-pril"
block ACE → ↓ angiotensin II → ↓ SVR · ↓ aldosterone → ↓ Na⁺/water · ↑ bradykinin
use: HTN (first-line) · HFrEF · after MI · CKD with albuminuria incl. diabetic kidney disease — "super duper renal protective" (dilates the efferent arteriole → ↓ glomerular pressure)
AE: hypotension (esp. at start) · hyperkalemia · dry cough (bradykinin) · angioedema · ↑ creatinine · fetal toxicity
nursing: BP, K⁺, renal function · angioedema → immediate evaluation, never again · never ACEI + ARB · caution with K⁺ supplements, K⁺-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs
✎ her pearl: the cough isn't infection — just "so incredibly irritating" that many people stop the drug. Angioedema (mouth, lips, airway) "is going to be considered an allergy."

Creatinine: a small bump when starting is expected — "we recognize that it happens, and we are okay with it" because long term it saves the kidney. NSAIDs constrict the afferent arteriole; wide-open outflow + little inflow is bad for the kidney.

ARBs · losartan (Cozaar), valsartan — "-sartan"
block angiotensin II at AT₁ receptors → same ↓ SVR, ↓ aldosterone · no bradykinin rise
use: HTN first-line, HFrEF, CKD with albuminuria · the switch when ACEI cough is intolerable
AE: hypotension, hyperkalemia, ↑ creatinine, rare angioedema, fetal toxicity · avoid in pregnancy, avoid with ACEI

supraventricular
starts above the ventricles
AFib, flutter, SVT

atria → ventricles delayed or blocked

why dysrhythmias happen (slide 48)
  • automaticity (impulse formation): the SA node normally sets the rate; other cells fire at the wrong time/rate → premature beats, tachydysrhythmias; weak pacemaker → bradycardia
  • conduction block: impulses delayed or interrupted (AV block)
  • reentry: an impulse loops through a circuit → rapid, repetitive firing (many SVTs)
  • triggers: ischemia/MI, hypoxia, K⁺ and Mg²⁺ abnormalities, cardiac surgery/structural disease, medications incl. antiarrhythmics
student asked: does "impaired pacemaker activity" mean the SA node? → yes — the heart's own pacemaker.
FYI only — slide 46's long table and slide 47's action-potential details ("probably beyond… we're just going to go past this one"). Just know Na⁺, Ca²⁺ and K⁺ run cardiac conduction, so the drugs target those channels.
atrial fibrillation (slide 49)
chaotic atrial impulses — the atria literally fibrillate → irregularly irregular ventricular rhythm · stasis (esp. left atrial appendage) + turbulence → clots → stroke · fast rates ↓ filling & CO
three goals: rate (beta blocker, diltiazem/verapamil — slow the AV node) · rhythm (amiodarone, cardioversion, ablation) · stroke prevention (anticoagulation)
Kahoot: why does AFib increase stroke risk? → "when blood stops flowing or slows down, it starts to clot" — stasis + turbulence.
student asked: where does the clot go? → atria → ventricle → out; can lodge anywhere (renal artery), but the most dangerous is the brain → stroke; tiny ones show up as marks under the fingernails.
student asked: do patients feel AFib? → not always. And after > 24 hours in AFib you can't just cardiovert — a clot has almost certainly formed and the shock could break it free → anticoagulate first.
student asked: why the left atrial appendage? → it's a little out-pouching that makes clotting much easier; right-sided clots (→ lungs) happen far less.
atrial flutter (slide 50)
one organized reentry circuit in the atrium · 250–350/min but regular · sawtooth waves · AV node lets 2:1, 3:1, 4:1 through → "regularly irregular" · rate/rhythm control, anticoagulate like AFib
SVT (slide 51)
reentry usually through the AV node → regular, narrow, very fast · too fast (≈ 180–200) → no filling time → ↓ CO
stable: vagal maneuvers (bear down, cough) → adenosine → beta blocker/non-DHP · unstable: synchronized cardioversion
Kahoot: which finding is characteristic of atrial flutter? → regular but incredibly fast atrial activity (sawtooth) ("good").
student asked: could sustained SVT make blood back up? → "It could" — heart-failure signs, and over time hypertrophy; but it shouldn't go on that long.
✎ her pearl: babies can't "bear down" — with an order, a face-first dunk in ice water triggers the diving reflex.
student asked: can you stimulate the vagus through the neck? → yes, but carotid pressure can break a plaque free and cuts blood flow to the brain.
student asked: can recurring SVT be fixed? → rate control first; if they find the source, ablation (heat, freeze, laser) "can work very nicely."

8 · ventricular rhythms & AV block (Trax and Taylor)

VT (slide 52) — "one step away from dying"
rapid impulses from the ventricles · often ischemia, MI, structural disease · no filling time → hypotension, syncope, collapse → can become VF
treat by status: stable with pulse → IV amiodarone or procainamide · unstable with pulse → synchronized cardioversion · pulseless → CPR + unsynchronized defibrillation
synchronized cardioversion
the machine takes a moment to sync, then shocks at the exact right point of the cardiac cycle — when there's a rhythm (and a pulse)
unsynchronized defibrillation
pulseless — "there's nothing to synchronize to," just shock
VF (slide 53)
chaotic ventricular activity → no effective CO = cardiac arrest · immediate CPR + unsynchronized defibrillation · epinephrine, amiodarone or lidocaine per the arrest algorithm · find the cause
drugs support resuscitation; they do NOT replace CPR and defibrillation
Kahoot: which treatment is essential for ventricular fibrillation? → VF is cardiac arrest — CPR + unsynchronized defibrillation.
PVCs (slide 54)
an ectopic ventricular beat early, wide QRS · common, often benign — "every once in a while it's allowed to have a [bad] beat" · triggers: stimulants/SNS, ischemia, ↓K⁺, ↓Mg²⁺ · frequent → ↓ efficiency · treat the cause, fix electrolytes; beta blockers if symptomatic
her night-shift PVCs (caffeine) went away when she came off nights
FYI only — the "more than six per minute" PVC cutoff: "you usually don't memorize this."
torsades de pointes (slide 55)
polymorphic VT from a prolonged QT → can become VF · "party streamer" in French — smaller, bigger, smaller, bigger
risks: QT-prolonging drugs (incl. antiarrhythmics) · ↓K⁺, ↓Mg²⁺ · congenital long QT · bradycardia
Tx: IV magnesium sulfate — even when Mg²⁺ is normal · fix electrolytes, stop QT drugs · unstable → defibrillate
Kahoot: primary medication treatment for torsades de pointes? → magnesium. "The rest of them you don't need to know except for adenosine for SVT and magnesium for torsades."

AV node = a built-in pause so the ventricles can fill. AV blocks show up in the PR interval.

1st degree
PR too long but constant; every P conducts
Taylor is always late — by the same amount — but always shows up
2nd · Mobitz I
PR longer, longer, longer → a dropped beat → repeat
later each night until one night she doesn't come home
2nd · Mobitz II
PR doesn't change, then beats drop without warning
usually on time, then sometimes just doesn't show
3rd degree
complete AV dissociation; ventricles ~40/min on their own → pacemaker
completely separate lives
✎ her trick: Trax = the P wave (atria), Taylor = the QRS (ventricles). Normal: Trax gets home first; Taylor comes home after, at the same time every night.
Kahoot: progressive PR prolongation before a dropped beat indicates… → second-degree Mobitz type I.
pharm connection (slide 56)
beta blockers, verapamil, diltiazem and digoxin slow the AV node → can worsen AV block · symptomatic/high-grade block may need pacing
FYI only — "PR interval is greater than 0.20 seconds. You don't need to know that." Mobitz names: "just know second degree type one, second degree type two."

Kahoot: which antiarrhythmic class primarily blocks potassium channels? → class III ("good, good").
  • lidocaine (slide 58): blocks fast Na⁺ channels, calms ischemic ventricular tissue · selected ventricular dysrhythmias, refractory VF/pulseless VT · IV · monitor ECG, BP, neuro status
  • metoprolol (slide 59): ↓ SA automaticity, ↓ AV conduction → rate control in AFib/flutter · same bradycardia/hypotension/bronchospasm cautions
  • diltiazem/verapamil (slide 61): slow the SA & AV nodes · rate control · can worsen HFrEF · dihydropyridines (amlodipine) are NOT used for dysrhythmias
amiodarone (Pacerone, Cordarone) — "so many toxicities"
prolongs repolarization & refractory period (K⁺) + Na⁺, Ca²⁺, β effects · atrial dysrhythmias, VT, refractory VF/pulseless VT
toxicity: pulmonary (pneumonitis, fibrosis) · thyroid (hypo or hyper) · liver · bradycardia, QT prolongation · corneal deposits, photosensitivity, skin discoloration
long-term → monitor thyroid, liver, lungs · many interactions · very long half-life
"a really, really crappy drug to be taking" — but it works on some bad rhythms
Kahoot: which toxicity is important with long-term amiodarone? → pulmonary fibrosis — plus liver; "the big ones that we worry about."
adenosine (slides 62–63) — "turning it off and on again"
activates AV-node adenosine receptors → briefly blocks AV conduction → interrupts the reentry circuit · regular, AV node–dependent SVT
half-life < 10 seconds → rapid IV push + immediate saline flush (two people, stopcock) · continuous ECG, crash equipment, pads per protocol
AE: transient flushing, chest discomfort, dyspnea · brief bradycardia/AV block (a flat line, then the rhythm returns) · bronchospasm · warn the client · avoid AV nodal blockers in pre-excited AFib
Kahoot: [adenosine] can terminate some SVTs by… (drug name garbled) → briefly blocking AV-node conduction, breaking the reentry loop.
Kahoot: how should adenosine be given for SVT? → rapid IV push, then an immediate saline flush.
student asked: are they awake? → usually; they feel everything slow and stop, then better — the video called it "15 seconds of terror"; for the nurse, too: "even though you know it's going to come back… is this the time that it doesn't?"
★ proarrhythmia (slide 65)
every antiarrhythmic can cause dysrhythmias — "when you start messing with those channels… sometimes you can make things worse." QT-prolonging drugs → torsades.

10 · pericardium, valves & vessels

pericardium (slide 67)
acute pericarditis: inflamed sac (idiopathic/viral, autoimmune, after MI or surgery) · sharp, pleuritic chest pain, better leaning forward · friction rub · "every beat, you've got this inflamed sac that's getting stretched"
effusion: fluid builds in the sac (inflammation, cancer, trauma, infection)
tamponade: the tough sac doesn't stretch → squeezes the heart in diastole → ↓ filling → ↓ SV → ↓ CO · life-threatening → pericardiocentesis
constrictive: thick, fibrotic sac (after pericarditis, chest radiation) restricts filling → venous congestion, HF signs; less urgent than tamponade
Kahoot: cardiac tamponade decreases CO primarily by… → (slide 67) ↓ ventricular filling (→ ↓ stroke volume).
FYI only — pericarditis drugs (NSAIDs, colchicine): "you don't need to worry too much about the treatments."
stenosis
valve won't open fully → chamber pushes harder → pressure overload → hypertrophy
regurgitation
valve won't close → backflow → volume overload → dilation

aneurysm vs dissection (slide 73)
aneurysm: localized dilation of a weakened wall (HTN, atherosclerosis, smoking, connective tissue) · fusiform (whole circumference) vs saccular/berry (a neck + pouch — easier to clip) · rupture, thrombus/emboli, compression
dissection: intimal tear → blood between the layers → false lumen → rupture or cut-off flow (her example: neck manipulation → dissection → stroke)
DVT (slide 74) — "one of the scariest things"
Virchow's triad: venous stasis (immobility) · endothelial injury (trauma, surgery) · hypercoagulability (cancer, pregnancy, estrogen)
unilateral swelling, pain, warmth · embolizes → PE · post-thrombotic syndrome
anticoagulants stop the clot growing and ↓ embolization — they do not dissolve it; clot busters aren't used for DVT
she asked: "if it breaks free from the leg, where does it go?" → up to the lungs — a PE; one of the main causes of death in hospitalized clients.

11 · cardiomyopathies & endocarditis

dilated (DCM)
dilated ventricle, weak systolic squeeze → ↓ EF & CO · genetic, alcohol, cardiotoxic drugs, myocarditis → HF, dysrhythmias
hypertrophic (HCM)
thick muscle, often inherited → impaired relaxation & filling · dysrhythmias, sudden cardiac death
✎ her pearl: the healthy high-school athlete who collapses — "usually because of undiagnosed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy." Volunteers now EKG whole high schools in a day.
student asked: why don't primary care offices just do EKGs? → "I don't know" — probably the machine and the extra expense.
FYI only — HCM's dynamic LV outflow obstruction: "don't worry about."
myocarditis
inflamed myocardium (viral or immune) → ↓ contractility, dysrhythmias · may resolve or progress to DCM
COVID-vaccine myocarditis in some teen boys: all but one had a completely normal return of function
stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy — "broken heart syndrome"
intense emotional/physical stress → catecholamine surge → transient stunning, regional wall-motion abnormality · chest pain, dyspnea, ECG changes, ↑ biomarkers — mimics MI, but no coronary obstruction explains it · usually reversible
takotsubo = a Japanese octopus pot — the ventricle takes its shape
infective endocarditis (slide 78)
infection of the endocardium, mostly the valves · microbes stick to damaged tissue → vegetations = microorganisms + platelets + fibrin
risks: prosthetic or abnormal valves (e.g. MVP) · injection drug use (bacteria straight into the blood) · prior endocarditis · intravascular devices, bloodstream infections
consequences: valve destruction → regurgitation → HF · vegetation emboli → stroke/infarction ("septic emboli") · persistent bacteremia
Tx: long, pathogen-directed antibiotics (per blood cultures & susceptibility) ± valve surgery
Kahoot: in infective endocarditis, vegetations can cause… → (slide 78) emboli → stroke or systemic infarction (and valve destruction).
FYI only — the pathogen list ("you don't need to memorize them") and the Calgary endocarditis flowchart (slide 79).

tamponade/constriction → can't fill · stenosis → pressure, regurgitation → volume · PAD → perfusion, CVI → pooling · DVT → PE · DCM → systolic, HCM → filling · Takotsubo → stress, reversible
