2 · nociception in 4 steps

Nociception = the body sensing and signaling actual or potential tissue damage. Nociceptors (skin, muscle, connective tissue, circulation, abdominal/pelvic/thoracic viscera) detect it. "Good to remember them. Not to go too much into detail."

1TRANSDUCTION — painful stimulus → action potential at the receptor. Injury releases K⁺, H⁺, lactate, histamine, serotonin, bradykinin, prostaglandins, substance P → enough change in membrane potential → action potential. "it starts with the periphery"
2TRANSMISSION — the action potential travels to the spinal cord and up to the brain (Aδ and C fibers)
3PERCEPTION — brain receives and interprets: thalamus = "the relay station, the radio station"; somatosensory cortex = exact location, intensity, quality
4MODULATION — the body changes the intensity: nociceptive inhibition (endorphins, enkephalins; exogenous = morphine) + rubbing, cold, pressure, acupuncture, biofeedback, TENS. "If all the pain signals kept going to the brain, your brain will fry out."
hyperalgesia — overreaction to a painful stimulus: "a little bit of stimuli" → amplified response
allodynia — a non-painful stimulus is felt as painful: "your clothing on your skin, air brushing against your face"
Aδ fibersC fibers
structuremyelinatedunmyelinated
amount10%90%
stimulithermal, mechanicalpolymodal
speedfast 5–10 m/sslow 0.6–2 m/s
feelssharp, stinging, cuttingdull, burning, aching
contralateral organization ✎
one side of the brain handles signals from the opposite side of the body.
"The right finger is cut… the left side of the brain is going to detect that injury."
ipsilateral = same side · contralateral = opposite. "I have seen questions on it… I might ask you on it."
threshold vs tolerance ✎
threshold = stimulation needed to perceive pain — "very even across individuals"
tolerance = how much one will bear before seeking relief — varies widely: age, culture ("in our household, men don't cry"), past pain, sleep deprivation
expression varies too (pacing, writhing, jaw clenching, grimacing, guarding, crying, moaning) → "a good pain assessment is vital!"

tissue injury → resolves when it heals, usually within 3 months

CHRONIC / NEUROPATHIC · slide 17
tissue injury no longer present — no longer protective, self-perpetuating, hyperalgesia

visceral · slide 16
internal organs (chest, abdomen) · dull, aching, squeezing · low density of nociceptors · poorly localized ("it hurts all over") · contributes to referred pain
referred · slide 16
felt at a site different from the point of origin but innervated by the same spinal segment · common with visceral pain

6 · good COX / bad COX, NSAIDs & aspirin

Non-opioids (slide 23): acetaminophen · NSAIDs (ibuprofen, meloxicam, Celebrex, diclofenac, ketorolac/Toradol) · steroids (MDP). Adjuvants: gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin) · muscle relaxers (baclofen, tizanidine/Zanaflex, cyclobenzaprine/Flexeril) · SNRIs & TCAs (duloxetine/Cymbalta, venlafaxine/Effexor, amitriptyline, nortriptyline) · topicals (lidocaine, NSAIDs, capsaicin).

✎ "Remember the difference between the classes" — gabapentin is best for chronic/neuropathic pain; there Tylenol "doesn't do anything."
cyclooxygenase (COX) arachidonic acid → prostaglandins
COX-1 · "good COX"
all tissues, housekeeping: protects the gastric mucosa · supports renal function · platelet aggregation (thromboxane A₂ → platelets clump)
COX-2 · "bad COX"
made at sites of injury → inflammation, sensitizes pain receptors · brain: fever, pain perception · kidneys: renal function · vessels: vasodilation · colon: can contribute to cancer
block…harmfulbeneficial
COX-1gastric erosion, ulcers, bleeding, renal impairmentprotection against MI and stroke (less thromboxane → less platelet aggregation) — why aspirin is given after a heart attack
COX-2renal impairment, MI and stroke (no vasodilation → vasoconstriction)less inflammation, pain and fever

"Remember what COX-1 does, COX-2 does… then it's easier to understand" what blocking them does. "Ibuprofen is my favorite drug on the planet."

1st generation
block COX-1 and COX-2: aspirin, ibuprofen (Advil), naproxen (Aleve), ketorolac (Toradol) · anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antipyretic · all can cause GI ulcers, bleeding, renal impairment · non-aspirin ones: ↑ MI/stroke (unlike aspirin) → lowest dose, shortest time · safety not established in pregnancy
2nd generation (coxibs)
COX-2 only: celecoxib (Celebrex) · made to cut GI side effects → lower GI-bleed risk (ulcers still possible) · ↑ MI, stroke — avoid in heart disease, renal impairment · sulfonamide allergy (sulfur molecule) · contraindicated 3rd trimester · ↑ warfarin effect · uses OA, RA, acute pain, dysmenorrhea
Slide 26 and the study guide say 2nd-gen NSAIDs "inhibit COX-1 only" — likely a typo. Slide 28 and her words say COX-2: "the second generation will inhibit COX-2 only"; "Celebrex… is a COX-2 inhibitor."
"Celebrex is a wonder drug" for inflammation — but vasoconstriction → MI/stroke, "you hear this in the news."
after vein surgery: meloxicam for 7 days — "a hard stop at seven days."
NSAID / aspirin uses · slide 26
mild–moderate pain (joint, muscle, headache, post-op; not severe visceral pain) · fever · inflammation (RA, OA, rheumatic fever, tendinitis, bursitis — higher doses) · dysmenorrhea (less uterine prostaglandin)
aspirin: one dose suppresses platelets for the platelet's life (8 days) → stop 1 week before planned invasive procedures
✎ "I took my aspirin yesterday" → elective procedure cancelled, "no doubt." (Vascular surgery keeps aspirin — but remember most elective procedures.)
aspirin adverse effects · slide 27
  • GI: distress, heartburn, nausea (worse with age); long-term, even low dose → ulcers, perforation, bleeding (↑ acid/pepsin, ↓ mucus & bicarbonate, ↓ blood flow, direct irritation)
  • bleeding: contraindicated in bleeding disorders; stop ≥ 1 week before surgery; interacts with anticoagulants, steroids, other NSAIDs, vaccines
  • renal impairment · rare hypersensitivity · erectile dysfunction
  • salicylism (toxicity): tinnitus, sweating, headache, dizziness
  • Reye's syndrome → don't give aspirin to children
  • contraindicated in pregnancy
Reye's details (encephalopathy, liver degeneration, 20–30% mortality): "We're not going to go into details."

7 · Tylenol overdose: "definitely on the exam"

acetaminophen blocks COX in the CNS only → analgesic + antipyretic, NOT anti-inflammatory; doesn't affect platelets, cause GI bleeding or affect the kidneys.

acetaminophen → major pathway
~90%, nontoxic, out in urine
minor P450 pathway
5–10% · induced by ALCOHOL → toxic NAPQI → glutathione neutralizes it
depleted by ALCOHOL and OVERDOSE

"Do remember NAPQI" (not the full name). Too much Tylenol → more NAPQI → "the liver is not able to keep up" → NAPQI damages hepatic cells. Alcohol "uses the same pathway" → assess alcohol use before giving Tylenol.

the numbers ✎
liver-injury risk ↑ above 4,000 mg/day
"3,000 to 4,000 depending on what literature… I saw more like 4,000"
undernourished (low glutathione) → ≤ 3,000 mg/day
won't stop drinking → ≤ 2,000 mg/day
antidote acetylcysteine (NAC, Mucomyst, Acetadote) — replenishes glutathione → NAPQI harmless · within 8–10 h prevents injury ~100%, still protects up to 24 h
  • overdose = leading cause of acute liver failure in the US · early signs: N/V, diarrhea, sweating, abdominal pain → fulminant liver failure, death
  • adverse effects rare at normal doses (daily use linked to HTN, asthma; rare anaphylaxis, SJS, TEN)
  • interactions: alcohol (liver injury) · warfarin (↑ levels) · vaccines (blunts the immune response)
  • teach: liver risk, multiple products with acetaminophen, caution with liver disease

8 · the big guns: opioid classes & morphine

Opioids (narcotics) act like morphine; opiates come from the opium poppy; they mimic endogenous opioids (enkephalins, endorphins, dynorphins) at μ receptors → analgesia, sedation, euphoria, respiratory depression — "very important to know these."

pure agonist
activate μ and κ → analgesia, euphoria, sedation, respiratory depression, physical dependence, constipation
strong: morphine · moderate: codeine
agonist-antagonist
alone: low to moderate receptor activity · with a full agonist: blocks it
butorphanol (IM, nasal spray)
pure antagonist
blocks μ and κ → reverses CNS and respiratory depression
naloxone (Narcan) — slide's "Naracain"
morphine · Schedule II, strong
high abuse potential → severe physical/psychological dependence · Schedule II examples on the slide: morphine, oxycodone, Dilaudid, fentanyl, methadone — plus amphetamine (Adderall) and methylphenidate (Ritalin), which are Schedule II but not opioids
also drowsiness, less anxiety, sense of well-being · uses: severe pain — post-op, labor & delivery, cancer, MI
routes: IM, IV, PO, subQ, epidural, intrathecal · first-pass effect → oral dose much higher than IV
she asked: "What is the normal respiration rate? If somebody's breathing at eight, what are you going to do?" → 12–20 — "bring out your Narcan."
she asked: Morphine post-op — "get up and get going. Are you going to do that?" → no — sit them up first, slowly (orthostatic hypotension).
student asked: "What is Schedule II?" → "a higher potential for abuse."
morphine adverse effects — "I guarantee you… on NCLEX" · most seen: respiratory depression, constipation, sedation · "low and slow"
  • respiratory depression — most serious, can be deadly; rare at therapeutic doses; worse with other CNS depressants; tolerance develops with long use
  • constipation — ↓ GI contractions, ↑ anal sphincter tone, ↓ fluid secretion → impaction, perforation, rectal tearing, hemorrhoids (also why it can treat diarrhea)
  • orthostatic hypotension — blunts the baroreceptor reflex → change position slowly
  • pruritus (diphenhydramine) · urinary retention · cough suppression (cough at intervals, assess lung sounds) · biliary colic
  • emesis (stimulates the CTZ — antiemetic) · ↑ ICP from CO₂ buildup
  • euphoria (more relief, more addiction risk) or dysphoria
  • sedation — drowsiness, mental clouding; teach safety · miosis (pinpoint pupils, poor vision in dim light)
  • teratogenic · neurotoxicity (delirium, agitation, myoclonus — renal impairment, long use, cognitive impairment) · prolonged use: hormonal changes, immune suppression
tolerance, dependence, withdrawal · slide 37
  • tolerance: larger doses for the same effect — very pronounced; very little tolerance to constipation and miosis; cross-tolerance. Cancer patients "need a lot more" as the cancer spreads.
  • dependence: stopping → withdrawal → long-term patients taper
  • withdrawal: yawning, rhinorrhea, sweating, goosebumps → violent sneezing, weakness, N/V, cramps, bone/muscle pain, spasms
withdrawal takes 10 days — "very uncomfortable but not dangerous"
"you may have an NCLEX question, how long does withdrawal take"
precautions · slide 38
  • ↓ respiratory reserve: asthma, emphysema, kyphoscoliosis, obesity
  • labor & delivery (passes to the newborn) · head injury (↑ ICP, clouds the neuro exam)
  • infants and elderly: extra sensitive to respiratory depression · BPH: more urinary retention
student asked: "Do adverse effects or dependence vary by route? Do we need the dosages?" → depends on how long they've taken it, not the route; dosages — no.
FYI only: slide 38's interaction list (anticholinergics worsen constipation/retention; antihypertensives worsen hypotension). Know from slide 34 that an agonist-antagonist given to someone on a long-term full agonist can trigger withdrawal.

10 · PCA (only the patient presses), naloxone, buprenorphine

PCA · "very important"
self-dosing as needed, with limits (dose + delay, e.g. a set dose every 10 minutes) · often a loading dose
ONLY THE PATIENT presses the button — teach family never to press it
reassure: the machine is set so they can't overdose themselves (lockout) — yet respiratory depression is still possible → frequent sedation & RR checks · verify drug/concentration/programming per policy · compare demands vs delivered doses · evaluate relief and function
naloxone (Narcan)
competitive antagonist → reverses opioid overdose · no effect without opioids on board · IV, IM, subQ, nasal
half-life shorter than most opioids → may need more than one dose · precipitates immediate withdrawal in opioid-dependent patients
nursing: support airway & ventilation · reassess RR & LOC often · watch for re-sedation
priority: restore adequate ventilation, not completely eliminate analgesia
buprenorphine · partial μ agonist
Belbuca, Subutex, Suboxone, IV · low intrinsic activity, high affinity · risk of precipitating withdrawal · medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD), also a potent analgesic
ceiling effect → less respiratory depression · less sedation, less euphoria · long half-life (SL/buccal 24–42 h) · fewer withdrawal symptoms · less opioid-induced hyperalgesia · lower misuse
